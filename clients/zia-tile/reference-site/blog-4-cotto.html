Introducing Cotto: History, Applications, and Care

Our Cotto begins with natural Mexican clay near San Miguel de Allende, which is hand-pressed into molds, sun-dried, and high-fired using traditional techniques. The result is a terra cotta defined by tonal variation, surface character, and the inherent details that give handmade tile its appeal. Over time, the unglazed surface develops a natural patina that reflects the material's connection to place, process, and age.

What Is Cotto Tile?

Cotto is Zia's terra cotta line. Terra cotta is Italian for "cooked," referencing a kiln-fired clay tile, traditionally unglazed, with tones determined entirely by the soil from which it came. Reds, burnt oranges, and soft browns are typical, as tones shift from one piece to the next in ways that give an installed floor its depth.

Zia's Cotto line was born from our three traditional tones: Adobe, Fired Earth, and Red Clay, while our newer whitewashed Blanco, deep brown Madera, and matte black Oscura round out the earthy palette.

The material has a long history in architecture, where clay-rich soil made it a foundational building material across farmhouses, monasteries, and villas. The floors absorbed heat during the day and released it slowly, which supported comfort in warmer climates. Many historic installations are still in use today.

Honed through generations of tile-making, the process has not changed much: clay is hand-pressed into molds, sun-dried in open courtyards for one to two weeks, and high-fired in traditional kilns.

Three qualities define our Cotto collection:

  • An unglazed clay body with tonal variation from piece to piece
  • Dense kiln firing that strengthens the material
  • A porous surface that develops patina with use and time

Use and time deepen the tone. The installation is a starting point, not a forever state. Variation in size, color, shade, and surface texture is a natural characteristic of terra cotta, to be expected in every Cotto order, and part of the reason a finished Cotto surface reads as handmade.

Cotto, Saltillo, and Cotto Allende: How They Compare

Cotto, Saltillo, and Cotto Allende are all clay tiles, and the differences between them shape where each one belongs.

Cotto and Terra Cotta

Terra cotta is a broader category of tile, describing a wide range of fired clay materials. Cotto specifically refers to our distinct kiln-fired, traditionally unglazed clay tile with a denser body and a more refined surface than rustic terra cotta. Our Red Clay Cotto is the most direct expression of that tradition in our line.

Cotto comes in two colorway groups: three traditional terra cotta hues and three more unexpected tones.

The traditional colorways carry the warm range that has defined terra cotta for centuries:

  • Adobe: the lightest of the three, a soft, sun-bleached tone
  • Fired Earth: the mid-range classic warm terra cotta. Wood ash applied to the surface during firing produces the distinct textured coloration that sets it apart from the other traditional hues
  • Red Clay: the traditional, deepest red, closest to the raw material itself

The contemporary colorways extend the collection beyond what most expect from terra cotta:

  • Blanco: a whitewashed finish that brightens and softens the body's natural tone
  • Madera: a deep cocoa, one of the few dark brown terra cotta tiles produced anywhere
  • Oscura: a matte black terra cotta, rare in unglazed clay

The range of Cotto shapes and sizes is part of why the line lands in a contemporary kitchen as comfortably as in a Spanish courtyard. Standard squares (4×4, 8×8, 13×13) and rectangles (2×6, 6.5×13) anchor the line, alongside an 8×9 hexagon and a set of signature shapes drawn from Spanish-colonial and Mesoamerican design traditions:

  • Stars & Cross (6×6) — an eight-pointed star paired with a cross, the two shapes interlocking into a Moorish pattern found in courtyards from Granada to Puebla
  • Alcazar (6×6) and Big Alcazar (13×13) — a geometric interlocking shape with curved and straight edges, named for the alcázar, the Spanish-Moorish fortress, offered in two sizes
  • Oaxaca (7.5×13) — an hourglass shape paired with rounded octagons; named for the Mexican state and its UNESCO-listed capital
  • Durango (10×10.5) — a four-tile set that builds an abstract geometric pattern across a floor; named for the silver-mining country of northern Mexico
  • Tornillo (3.25×13) — long, narrow parallelograms set in a staggered overlapping layout; tornillo is Spanish for "screw"
  • Toltec (8×13) — a trapezoid format recalling a yin and yang, named for the pre-Aztec Mesoamerican civilization centered at Tula
  • Zocalo (6.5×13) — a striking asymmetrical puzzle design, with two pieces forming a unified rectangle

Saltillo

Saltillo is a clay tile produced in Saltillo, Coahuila, in northern Mexico; a different region and a different process from Cotto. Saltillo is largely sun-dried with limited kiln finishing, producing a softer, more porous body that absorbs moisture readily and is prone to efflorescence, staining, and surface wear without consistent resealing.

The color range tends toward inconsistent peaches, pinks, and oranges, with frequent irregularities from the drying process. These characteristics are why Saltillo is generally reserved for decorative or low-traffic interior applications, while Cotto holds up across kitchens, entryways, commercial floors, freeze/thaw exterior installations, and properly sealed showers.

Cotto Allende

Cotto Allende is our glazed terra cotta tile, beginning with the same clay foundation as Cotto, hand-hewn and high-fired, then hand-glazed. The line takes its name from where it is produced near San Miguel de Allende, Mexico, though Allende also means "beyond," as the glazes introduce color and texture beyond the spectrum of natural clay. Finishes range from matte to glossy, with speckling that varies by colorway, with subtle crazing and color shifts.

The collection features eighteen colorways, with names that reference the geography and flora of the line's Mexican origin: Sayulita (the Pacific surf town), Cacao, Creosote and Peyote (the desert plant and the cactus), Arroyo (a dry creek), Condesa (the Mexico City neighborhood), Pedregal (rocky ground). Four shapes ground the line: 4×4 square, 4×8 rectangle, triangle, and the 1.5×8 mini bar — our narrowest format, useful for linear or stack-bond layouts.

Where Cotto Belongs

Terra cotta performs best in spaces where warmth, texture, and natural aging are part of the design intention. It is a material that rewards the right placement.

Interior Floors

Interior floors are where Cotto is most at home. Larger formats — 13×13 squares, the 8×9 hexagon, or the bolder Big Alcazar — anchor open layouts; Stars & Cross and the smaller 6×6 Alcazar work in compact rooms where the interlocking patterns read at scale. Foot traffic deepens the tone and softens the surface over time, and living rooms, kitchens, and entryways all benefit. The material anchors the architecture without competing with anything else in the room.

Walls and Backsplashes

On vertical surfaces, the tonal variation within the clay becomes the main event. The 2×6 rectangle reads classical for kitchen backsplashes; Tornillo's staggered parallelograms build a more contemporary pattern, and Cotto Allende's 1.5×8 mini bar suits linear or stack-bound layouts. Fireplace surrounds and accent walls gain depth through natural shade shifts and matte texture. Light interacts gently with the surface, creating dimension without a heavy pattern.

Bathrooms and Showers

Cotto is suitable for shower walls and floors in bathrooms and requires proper sealing. Smaller formats — 4×4 squares or the 6×6 Alcazar — build more grout joints into the floor, which is useful for slip resistance. Apply 511 Porous Plus before grouting and plan to reseal periodically to maintain moisture protection.

Exterior Floors and Outdoor Spaces

This is one of the unglazed Cotto's real advantages: it performs in any climate, including freeze/thaw regions. Patios, courtyards, garden paths, and outdoor kitchens all work — Big Alcazar and Stars & Cross suit larger expanses, while standard 8×8 or 13×13 squares anchor more contained outdoor rooms.

Cotto's exterior sealing protocol is two-step: apply 511 Porous Plus Penetrating Sealer before grouting in thin layers until the tile reaches full saturation, then apply a second coat of 511 Porous Plus after the grout has cured. Fila Matte Wax is reserved for interior Cotto installations only, as UV exposure discolors the wax over time.

One application unglazed Cotto cannot handle is full submersion. For pools and spas, Cotto Allende is the right choice within the line, as the glaze seals the surface to a level the unglazed body cannot reach. Cotto Allende itself is not suitable for outdoor freeze/thaw installations, so the two products divide the outdoor landscape cleanly: unglazed Cotto for cold-climate exteriors and patios, Cotto Allende for pools and spas.

Sunrooms and Transitional Spaces

Clay responds well to natural light. The matte finish reduces glare while the warm tones shift subtly as the sun moves across the surface throughout the day. The traditional colorways of Adobe, Fired Earth, and Red Clay read most at home in this register. Paired with wood, plaster, and greenery, Cotto reinforces the grounded, indoor-outdoor character that makes transitional rooms work.

Commercial and Hospitality Settings

Commercial interiors benefit from the lived-in character of aging clay surfaces. Restaurants and retail spaces use Cotto flooring to build atmosphere and character. Larger formats (13×13 squares, Big Alcazar) anchor expansive commercial floors; Stars & Cross or the smaller Alcazar build a more intimate scale for boutique spaces. A consistent sealing and maintenance schedule keeps the surface performing well under heavier traffic.

How to Care for Cotto

Maintaining Cotto is simple, but consistency is what keeps the surface performing over time. The essentials:

  • Seal before and after grouting using 511 Porous Plus. For interior applications, finish with Fila Matte Wax as a final protective coat
  • Clean with pH-neutral products such as Miracle Sealants pH Neutral Tile and Stone Cleaner
  • Avoid vinegar, bleach, and abrasive scrubbers, as these degrade sealers and damage the clay surface over time
  • Reseal based on traffic and moisture exposure. Wet installations should be resealed annually at minimum; high-traffic interior floors will need more frequent attention than light-use rooms
  • Clean spills promptly. The porous clay body absorbs moisture quickly, and stains that sit become harder to remove

How to Install Cotto Tile

Good installation comes down to preparation and sequencing, and the most important thing to know up front is this: the pre-grout sealer is doing two jobs at once. It is protecting the porous clay, and it is also acting as a grout release. Skip it or rush it, and the grout will pull stain into the body of the tile permanently.

Before anything goes down, blend tiles from all boxes and lay them out dry across the surface. Cotto varies in tone and size from piece to piece, and distributing that variation before setting is what makes the finished floor feel considered rather than random.

The subfloor must be fully cured, stable, and clean before starting. The core installation steps are:

  • Wet the back of each tile before applying high-quality medium-bed thinset mortar
  • Set tiles with consistent 1/8" grout joints
  • Use a rubber mallet to compact tiles evenly and ensure a level surface
  • Allow the thinset to cure for at least 48 hours before sealing
  • Apply 511 Porous Plus before grouting and allow it to fully cure (typically 24–48 hours). The sealer doubles as a grout release. Do not proceed until it has cured
  • Grout with Mapei Flexcolor CQ in small sections and clean as you go
  • For interior applications, finish with Fila Matte Wax as a final protective coat
  • For exterior applications, apply an additional coat of 511 Porous Plus instead of wax

For exposed edges, finish with mitered edges, Schluter strips, or bring drywall flush with the finished tile surface. If anything in the install sequence is unclear for your specific project, our team works through these details with installers regularly — info@ziatile.com.

Frequently Asked Questions About Cotto Tile

Is Cotto tile durable?

Yes, when properly sealed and maintained. The kiln firing process strengthens the clay, making terra cotta suitable for residential floors, walls, exterior applications in any climate, and light commercial use. Our Cotto's DCOF rating of 0.98 sits well above the 0.42 threshold typically required for level interior floors. How long it lasts depends largely on how consistently it is sealed and cared for.

Is Cotto compatible with underfloor heating?

Yes. Clay-rich tile is excellent at absorbing heat through the day and releasing it slowly. Cotto can be set over a properly designed radiant system; standard practice for porous tile applies, including a fully cured slab and a gradual temperature ramp after installation.

What's Cotto's slip resistance?

Cotto's DCOF rating is 0.98; this is well above the 0.42 threshold typically required for level interior floors. Slip resistance can be increased further by widening the grout joint or applying an anti-slip product after installation, especially useful in showers.

Does Cotto tile need sealing?

Yes, and the sequence matters more than installers expect. Apply 511 Porous Plus before grouting and allow it to fully cure. The sealer doubles as a grout release, and grouting before it has cured will pull stains into the clay permanently. After grouting with Mapei Flexcolor CQ, finish with Fila Matte Wax for interior applications. Wet installations should be resealed annually.

Can Cotto tile be used in bathrooms?

Yes, Cotto can be used in bathrooms. Bathroom floors, walls, and showers all suit Cotto when sealed correctly. Apply 511 Porous Plus before grouting and plan to reseal annually for wet installations.
