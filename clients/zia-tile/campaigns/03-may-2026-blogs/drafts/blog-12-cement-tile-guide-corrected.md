# Encaustic Cement Tile: Handmade, Mineral-Pigmented, and Never Fired

Cement tile is a hand-pressed, never-fired tile in which mineral pigments are set into a surface layer rather than sitting on top as a glaze. The technique was developed in mid-19th-century France and spread through France and Spain before reaching Vietnam. Zia's cement tile collection is hand-produced there today using this 19th-century French method.

## What Cement Tile Is Made Of

The tile begins with mineral pigments and cement. The pigmented surface layer runs through the top of the tile rather than sitting on the surface as an applied finish. Below the pigmented layer, a structural cement backing provides the tile's body.

The pigment-through-surface composition gives cement tile its matte finish and holds pattern detail under wear. The same composition allows the surface to develop patina with wear, rather than reveal an unpatterned substrate beneath.

## Key Features of Encaustic Cement Tile

"Encaustic cement tile" is the unified term for the same material: hand-pressed, unfired, with mineral pigments set into a surface layer rather than applied as a glaze on top. 

The contemporary tile market also sells "encaustic-look" ceramic and porcelain tiles. These are kiln-fired tiles with surface-applied patterns that approximate the visual character of cement tile but use a different construction. They are not encaustic in the technical sense: the pigment is a coating, not a body-deep layer.

The pigment-through-surface composition is also what produces cement tile's inherent variation and patina behavior. No two tiles are identical. Inherent variation in tone and surface character (minor color shifts across the production run, mold marks visible at close range) is part of the material. Cement is a soft material, and the matte surface scratches and softens with wear. 

## Hand-Poured, Hydraulically Pressed, Air-Cured

The production sequence dates back to the 1850s in France and Catalonia and follows the same method today:

1. A metal mold placed on the tile face holds the pattern divisions in raised metal.

2. Artisans pour pigmented cement into each section one color at a time, forming the decorative surface layer.

3. A structural cement backing is poured behind the pigment layer to form the tile body.

4. A hydraulic press compresses the assembly, bonding the pigment layer to the backing.

5. The tile is soaked briefly in water, then cured on drying racks. It is never fired.

The hydraulic press is what bonds the pigment layer to the cement backing. The pigment becomes part of the tile rather than a coating over it. The curing process is what gives the cement its structural integrity without a kiln stage.

## Where Cement Tile Lands

Cement tile installs across most residential and commercial interior contexts. The DCOF of greater than 1 supports high-traffic and wet-floor applications when properly sealed. The pigment-through-surface composition holds pattern depth in spaces where the floor or wall carries the design weight.

### Kitchen Floors

Cement tile suits kitchen floors where the floor is meant to be the design element. Recessed cabinetry, stone counters, and unlacquered metals read as supporting surfaces against a patterned floor. 

The Kepler, Madrid, and Manolo Sets establish architectural geometry across the room; solid 8x8 or 4x4 fields in deep tones ([Terra Cotta](https://ziatile.com/products/terra-cotta-cement-tile), [Canyon](https://ziatile.com/products/canyon-cement-tile)) work in kitchens where the floor should support natural materials without competing. Ensure floor installations have proper drainage. Prompt spill management matters in kitchens. Oils and acidic liquids penetrate under-sealed surfaces.

### Bathrooms and Powder Rooms

Powder rooms are among the strongest applications. A small geometric pattern in a guest-facing footprint reads as a deliberate architectural choice. 

For full bathroom floors, smaller formats (4x4) sit well in compact spaces; larger formats (8x8) suit primary bathrooms with more visual room. Shower walls work when properly sealed. Shower floors are supported by cement's high DCOF; set grout joints to a minimum of 1/8 inch for traction, with sealing per the install guide. Ensure floor installations have proper drainage.

### Entry Foyers and Hallways

Geometric cement tile entry floors follow a continuous tradition from 19th-century French and Catalan workshops and Victorian civic interiors. The pattern reads at the threshold scale and establishes the architectural register of the interior from the door inward. 

Multi-tile pattern sets reach their full repeat in hallway runs where smaller formats would read as fragments. The high-traffic register matches cement tile's DCOF and breaking strength. Ensure floor installations have proper drainage.

### Fireplace Surrounds

Fireplace surrounds suit cement tile as an accent surface. The vertical placement removes the foot-traffic and slip-resistance considerations that govern floors, and the matte finish reads at close range against the contrasting glow of the fire. Solid colors in saturated tones (Colorama Aubergine, Stars & Cross patterns) suit fireplace-surround installations. Cement tile cannot be installed inside the firebox itself. The material is not approved for direct flame exposure.

### Outdoor Use

Cement tile is approved for exterior installations in non-freeze/thaw climates: patios, courtyards, and exterior floors in mild regions. For exterior surfaces, use a penetrating sealer only; wax causes discoloration under UV exposure. In freeze/thaw climates, the porous cement matrix absorbs moisture that expands during freeze cycles and compromises the tile over time. 

For exterior floors in any climate, including freeze/thaw, [Cotto](https://ziatile.com/collections/cotto), Zia's terra cotta, is the primary recommendation.

Pools and spas are not suited to cement tile. Contact Zia's team to confirm material selection for those applications.

Wear protective face and eye coverings when working with thinset, sealants, and grout. See [Proposition 65 Warnings](https://ziatile.com/pages/proposition-65-warnings). Finish transitions with mitered edges or Schluter strips, and bring the drywall flush with the finished tile surface. Sealing is required as part of the installation in all spaces, including dry interior floors. For the full sealing sequence, format selection, and application-by-application guidance, see Zia's [Cement Tile Installation Guide](https://ziatile.com/pages/cement-installation-guide).

## Frequently Asked Questions About Cement Tile

### Is cement tile the same as encaustic tile?

Yes. Encaustic and cement tile are the same material: a hand-pressed, unfired tile with mineral pigments set into a surface layer. The term "encaustic" refers specifically to this production method. Modern "encaustic-look" [ceramic](https://ziatile.com/collections/ceramics) or porcelain tiles use a different construction and are not encaustic in the technical sense.

### What is cement tile made of?

Cement tile is made from mineral pigments and cement. The pigmented surface layer measures approximately 3 millimeters thick and bonds to a structural cement backing during hydraulic pressing. The tile is cured on drying racks; it is never fired in a kiln.

### Is cement tile fired in a kiln?

No. Cement tile is hydraulically pressed and cured on drying racks at ambient temperature. The absence of a kiln stage is the defining production distinction from ceramic, porcelain, and terra cotta tile.

### How is cement tile different from ceramic tile?

Ceramic tile is clay-bodied, glazed, and kiln-fired at high temperatures. Cement tile is cement and mineral pigments, hand-pressed and cured at room temperature without firing. On ceramic, the pattern sits in the glaze coat; on cement, the pattern runs through a pigmented surface layer, which is why patina develops with wear rather than wearing through to an unpatterned body.
