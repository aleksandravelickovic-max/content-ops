# Best Tiles for Showers: Design & Material Guide

The shower is the most technically demanding tile installation in a residential project. Exposure to continuous moisture, thermal cycling from steam, and direct barefoot contact on a wet surface are conditions requiring a high-performance material. Moisture requires sealing and absorption management. Steam requires thermal compatibility between the tile, the adhesive, and the substrate. Barefoot contact on a wet surface requires measurable, verifiable slip resistance, with a specified standard.

Selecting the wrong material compromises safety, as a glazed tile with inadequate slip resistance on a shower floor is a hazard from the day it is installed. Material failure is another common concern. For instance, a shower tile without proper moisture compatibility or sealing protocol absorbs water into the substrate and surrounding construction from the inside out, degrading the installation. 

Shower floors are subject to a slip-resistance standard: ANSI A137.1 sets the minimum Dynamic Coefficient of Friction (DCOF) at 0.42 for level interior wet floors, and 0.60 or higher for barefoot residential showers. Shower walls carry no DCOF requirement. The most compelling tile materials for shower walls (glazed zellige at DCOF 0.16, ceramics gloss at DCOF 0.30) require supplemental treatment on a shower floor. A tile that reads beautifully overhead cannot be placed underfoot without adequate preparation.

Our [shower tile collection](https://ziatile.com/pages/shower-collection) includes materials approved for shower floors and walls across several distinct categories: unglazed terra cotta, unglazed and glazed zellige, ceramics in three finishes, marble, limestone, terrazzo, glass mosaics, and cement tile. This detailed guide offers valuable insights on which materials perform best, with recommendations on products and colorways from our collection. 

## What Makes a Good Shower Tile?

A good shower tile performs across two surfaces with different technical requirements. Shower floor tile is governed by DCOF (Dynamic Coefficient of Friction): the ANSI A137.1 minimum for wet interior floors is 0.42, and the Tile Council of North America recommends 0.60 or higher for barefoot residential showers. 

Shower wall tile is not subject to a DCOF requirement. The governing factors are moisture resistance, steam approval, absorption rate, and performance in a continuously wet, enclosed environment.

### Shower Floors: Slip Resistance and Surface Science

DCOF is the primary technical measure for shower floor tile. ANSI A137.1 sets the minimum at 0.42 for level interior wet floors. The Tile Council of North America recommends 0.60 or higher for barefoot residential showers, where no footwear contributes additional grip, and a fall means a hard enclosure with no soft surfaces. The 0.42 standard is the minimum acceptable. The 0.60 recommendation is the practical target.

Surface finish is what drives DCOF. Glaze creates a smooth film over the tile body that reduces friction significantly. Unglazed surfaces expose the raw material directly — the clay body, the mineral aggregate, the stone — and generate substantially more grip. The difference between a DCOF of 0.98 and 0.16 comes from the presence or absence of that glaze layer, not the material beneath it.

What to look for in a shower floor tile:

* **DCOF Rating:** 0.60+ recommended for barefoot residential showers; 0.42 is the ANSI wet-floor minimum  
* **Surface Finish:** Unglazed materials consistently deliver higher DCOF than glazed equivalents of the same base material  
* **Grout Joint Width:** Grout lines add micro-edges that contribute to grip independently of the tile's DCOF; minimum 1/8" on wet floors  
* **Tile Format:** Smaller formats (4x4, mosaics) carry more grout per square foot than large-format tiles, increasing effective traction across the surface  
* **Anti-Slip Treatment:** Required for any tile below 0.42 DCOF; recommended for tiles between 0.42 and 0.60 in high-use showers  
* **Absorption Rate:** Higher-absorption materials require more rigorous sealing protocols; inadequate sealing on a porous tile allows moisture to penetrate the substrate from the inside

### Shower Walls: Steam Approval, Wet Reading, and Design Range

Shower walls carry no DCOF requirement. The governing concerns shift to moisture resistance, steam compatibility, sealing protocol, and how the material reads in a continuously wet environment.

Steam approval is distinct from standard moisture approval. Steam penetrates more aggressively than liquid water and creates thermal cycling that affects tile adhesion and grout differently from a standard shower. Not all moisture-approved materials carry steam approval — confirm compatibility before specifying for a steam shower enclosure.

What to look for in a shower wall tile:

* **Steam Approval:** Separate from moisture approval; required for steam shower environments specifically  
* **Wet Reading:** Materials perform differently under water than in a dry room — honed stone becomes more legible as moisture reveals the veining; glazed surfaces carry more dynamic depth; matte ceramics hold a consistent tone wet or dry  
* **Absorption Rate:** Determines the sealing commitment over the life of the installation; higher absorption means more frequent resealing  
* **Sealing:** All shower wall materials require sealing and resealing every one to two years in wet areas

The absence of a DCOF threshold on shower walls opens the full material range to design-led decisions. The question shifts from what is safe underfoot to how the material reads in wet, light-reflecting conditions across a day.

## What Are the Best Tiles for Shower Floors?

The best tiles for shower floors are unglazed materials with a DCOF at or above the 0.60 threshold recommended for barefoot wet surfaces. Unglazed surfaces expose the raw clay or mineral body directly, generating friction that glazed finishes cannot match. The glaze layer that produces visual depth on a wall tile is what reduces grip on a floor. 

Three materials in our line clear the 0.60 threshold without supplemental anti-slip treatment: Cotto at DCOF 0.98, Unglazed Natural Zellige at DCOF 0.84, and Ceramics Matte at DCOF 0.74.

### Cotto — The Highest DCOF in Our Shower Line at 0.98

Our Cotto is unglazed terra cotta produced from natural Mexican clay near San Miguel de Allende, hand-pressed into molds, sun-dried in open courtyards, and high-fired in traditional kilns. The unglazed surface registers a DCOF of 0.98: the highest in our collection, more than twice the ANSI wet-floor minimum, and nearly double the 0.60 threshold recommended for barefoot residential showers. 

On a shower floor, Cotto does not require anti-slip treatment, supplemental coating, or widened grout joints to perform safely. The material achieves it through what it is: raw, unglazed clay.

The same quality that produces that DCOF — the porous, matte surface of fired clay — also determines how Cotto reads and behaves in a wet environment. When moisture contacts the unglazed surface, the tone deepens slightly. In a functioning shower, the floor reads differently wet than dry in a way that a glazed surface does not. The terra cotta warms underfoot, and the matte surface holds water without reflecting it. 

Cotto develops patina in shower conditions as it does in any installation, though daily moisture and foot traffic accelerate the cycle. The surface deepens in tone along the most-used paths. A Cotto shower floor develops the specific depth that use and time build into unglazed clay, and that accumulation is part of what specifying this material in a shower delivers. The installation is a starting point, not a forever state.

### Unglazed Natural Zellige — DCOF 0.84

Unglazed natural zellige is a distinct category from glazed zellige in every technical specification that governs shower floor performance. The DCOF is 0.84. The surface, featuring raw Moroccan clay, is matte and textural, with inherent variation in color, edge, and thickness that defines a hand-formed tile. [Unglazed zellige](https://ziatile.com/blogs/resources/unglazed-natural-zellige-in-a-league-of-its-own) carries the character of a clay surface developed in the kiln and left as the fire shaped it, without a glaze layer mediating between the clay body and the space it occupies.

On a shower floor, unglazed zellige delivers Moroccan handmade character at a DCOF that clears the 0.60 barefoot residential threshold without supplemental treatment. What makes it specifically compelling as a shower floor is what distinguishes any handmade clay tile from a machine-made ceramic: each tile has its own character. A shower floor in [unglazed zellige](https://ziatile.com/products/unglazed-natural-zellige-tile) is not a uniform field. It reads as a composed material surface, with variation from tile to tile, in a way that daily barefoot use encounters differently every morning.

In a shower environment, the combination of daily moisture, the raw clay surface, and foot traffic builds patina into the installation differently from how it develops in a dry interior. The material ages into the shower as the shower ages into the house. Over the years, high-traffic paths deepen first, and the floor begins to document the specific pattern of habitation above it.

**Installation Note:** Do not soak unglazed zellige tiles before setting. The standard glazed zellige pre-installation soak causes efflorescence in the unglazed surface that is difficult to remove after the fact. Clean dust from the face and back with a damp cloth, then set it dry. 

Pre-grout sealing per the Installation Guide is mandatory; the sealant must cure fully before grouting proceeds. Our unglazed zellige is not suitable for pools or spas.

### Ceramics Matte — DCOF 0.74

Our ceramics are produced in the Nagoya region of central Japan from high-temperature-fired clay, molded and glazed before firing. The matte finish carries a DCOF of 0.74 and a water absorption rate of approximately 0.2%, which classifies it as near-impervious by ANSI standards. 

On a shower floor, these two figures together determine the maintenance commitment: ceramics offer sufficient grip to clear the 0.60 barefoot threshold without treatment, and low enough absorption to require the sealing protocol. Matte finish ceramics are substantially less demanding than terra cotta or natural stone.

The matte surface reads identically wet and dry. There is no tonal shift when moisture contacts it, no reflective change under shower light, no deepening of tone as the material absorbs what a glaze surface would deflect. On a shower floor, such consistency becomes a design asset when the floor is meant to recede, and allow the wall material or the fixtures to carry the design. The [Linen](https://ziatile.com/products/linen-4x4-ceramic-tile) colorway, a warm chalky neutral with a slightly textured surface typical of Japanese matte ceramics, suits both shower floor and wall applications.

[Field Trip Japan Etched](https://ziatile.com/collections/field-trip-japan-etched) carries a DCOF of 0.45 and is approved for shower floors in low-traffic residential applications. [Field Trip Japan Flat](https://ziatile.com/collections/field-trip-japan-flat) carries a DCOF of 0.40 and requires anti-slip treatment on shower floors.

Pre-grout sealing per the Installation Guide is required for ceramics matte in wet installations. The step prevents grout haze from bonding to the matte surface. On a satin or gloss ceramic, grout haze can be cleaned after the fact. On a matte surface, the sealer acting as a release agent is the only reliable way to keep the tile face clean through the grouting process.

## What Are the Best Tiles for Shower Walls?

The best tiles for shower walls are materials approved for continuous moisture and steam that carry visual depth in wet, enclosed conditions. Glazed zellige emerged as a popular shower wall tile in residential and hospitality design in 2025 and 2026, valued for the way its dynamic glazes intensify under running water and in the concentrated light of a shower enclosure. 

Honed marble, ceramics in matte, satin, and gloss finishes, limestone, terrazzo, cement tile, and glass mosaics are all strong shower wall tile options across different design registers. The right choice is determined by how the material reads in wet conditions and what sealing and maintenance commitment the installation requires.

The stone becomes more present in a shower, and this effect works because the honed surface allows moisture to interact with the stone directly. ### Ceramics — Three Finishes, Three Design Registers on Shower Walls

All three ceramic finishes — matte (DCOF 0.74), satin (DCOF 0.45), and gloss (DCOF 0.30) — are approved for shower walls, including steam showers. On shower walls, the finish choice is a design decision with no safety constraint.

Matte ceramics read quietly in wet conditions. The tone holds consistently whether the surface is dry, wet, or steam-covered. This predictability makes matte ceramics the right choice when the wall should provide structure and a neutral backdrop without competing for attention. A matte ceramic shower wall behind a statement fixture, or in a rectilinear enclosure where precision is the intention, holds its position in the composition without pulling focus. The [**Linen 2x8**](https://ziatile.com/products/linen-2x8-ceramic-tile) in a horizontal stack bond gives a shower wall architectural proportion. The [4x4](https://ziatile.com/products/linen-4x4-ceramic-tile) in a grid installation reads clean and controlled.

Satin ceramics carry a moderate sheen that changes as the light source and angle change. In a shower environment, with steam rising and overhead light shifting as the door opens and closes, a [satin ceramic](https://ziatile.com/collections/satin-ceramics?page=1) shower wall reads differently across a morning than a matte wall does. Designers working with satin ceramics in shower walls choose the finish precisely for that quality: the wall is present, not neutral.

Gloss ceramics in a shower enclosure maximize reflectivity. In a compact shower with limited natural light — a windowless bathroom, an interior apartment bathroom — [gloss ceramic tiles](https://ziatile.com/collections/glossy-ceramics) on the walls return overhead light across the full surface, and the perceived brightness of the space increases significantly. Gloss is among the most practical choices for light-limited shower spaces. On shower floors, DCOF 0.30 requires anti-slip treatment.

### Terrazzo, Cement Tile, and Glass Mosaics

Terrazzo is produced in steel molds from crushed marble and stone aggregate, hydraulically pressed and ground to a polished surface. [Terrazzo tiles](https://ziatile.com/collections/terrazzo) are approved for shower walls and floors. The ground surface carries embedded aggregate, with visible flecks of marble, stone, and mineral, which catches moisture and returns it as a low, even sheen in a shower context. On wet shower floors, a minimum 1/8" grout joint is required. Sealing is required. Available in 12x12 and 24x24 formats.

Cement tile is approved for shower walls and floors. However, the same porous mineral surface that develops patina across generations behaves differently in a shower environment: daily moisture contact is consistent and intensive, and the patina arc progresses more quickly than in a dry interior. Sealing for [cement tile](https://ziatile.com/collections/cement-sets) in shower applications is non-negotiable: pre-grout sealing per the Cement Tile Installation Guide, applied in thin layers until the surface reaches full saturation, allowed to cure fully, then grouting proceeds. 

A cement tile shower surface that goes into service without proper pre-grout sealing will absorb grout staining permanently on day one. The material's high porosity means the sealer is what stands between the mineral layer and permanent damage. Cement tile is not suitable for pools or spas, or for outdoor installation in freeze/thaw climates.

Glass mosaics from our Northern Spain collection are produced from 98% recycled glass using renewable energy, hand-placed on 12x12 grids in seven patterns. The DCOF of 0.42 sits at exactly the ANSI wet-floor minimum; shower floor installations require anti-slip treatment. On shower walls, the 0% absorption rate makes glass genuinely impervious. Epoxy grout is required in all [glass mosaic](https://ziatile.com/collections/glass-mosaics) shower installations. 

On a shower wall, glass returns light and water differently from any other material in the line: the semi-translucent surface catches shower light at the interior angles created by the mosaic pattern and creates a visual depth that does not exist in the dry installation.

### What is DCOF, and why does it matter for shower tiles?

DCOF stands for Dynamic Coefficient of Friction, and it measures the slip resistance of a tile surface under wet, dynamic foot contact. ANSI A137.1 sets the minimum for level interior wet floors at 0.42. The Tile Council of North America recommends 0.60 or higher for barefoot residential shower floors. 

Tiles below 0.42 are not considered safe for shower floors without anti-slip treatment. Tiles between 0.42 and 0.59 meet the ANSI standard but may benefit from additional safety measures in high-use showers. DCOF does not apply to shower walls.

### Can glazed zellige be used on a shower floor?

Glazed zellige carries a DCOF of 0.16, below the 0.42 ANSI wet-floor minimum. It can be installed on a shower floor when a minimum 1/8" grout joint is used, and anti-slip treatment is applied after installation — both steps are required, not optional.
