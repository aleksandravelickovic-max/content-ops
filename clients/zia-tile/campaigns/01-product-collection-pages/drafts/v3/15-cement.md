## About *[Locked]*

Cement tile was first developed by French and Catalan craftspeople in the 1850s as a more affordable alternative to stone mosaic, and the technique was carried to Vietnam decades later, where it has been preserved one tile at a time ever since. Zia's cement line is produced in those Vietnamese workshops, with the same hand-press and the same seven-day cure used since the original method was set down.

The line spans 68 colors across a deep catalog of patterns and solids, all in the matte finish and the depth of pigment that distinguish cement tile from any printed or surface-glazed alternative. Cement tile suits residential entryways and patterned bathroom floors, restaurant entries and patios, and the kind of long-life commercial floors that benefit from a tile designed to wear and re-seal across decades.

## Inherent Variation

- Color shifts subtly tile to tile within every colorway, with the deeper pigments carrying broader internal contrast than the lighter cream and bone tones.
- Each tile carries the hand of the maker in the pigment lay, the press, and the cure: minor crazing, slight irregularities at the edge, and small color drifts that read as a single soft matte field once the floor is laid.
- Expect tonal variation across the box, surface character that softens with patina, and small pits or air pockets in the surface, all natural to a hand-poured pigmented cement tile.

## Preparation and Curation

Open every box and blend tiles across the order before any thinset goes down. Lay out a dry run in the installation area, especially for patterns, to confirm the layout reads the way it should across the room.

We recommend a 15 to 20% overage on all cement tile orders to account for cuts and the natural variation across the line. For installations where a more uniform appearance is the priority, plan for a 25% overage so the installer can set aside the small share of tiles that drift furthest from the average. Cement tile carries a 10 to 12 week lead time on special order, so plan curation against the project schedule before placing the order.

## Installation and Finishing

Installers must wear protective face and eye coverings when working with cement tile, thinset, sealants, and grout, in accordance with [California Prop 65](https://www.ziatile.com/proposition-65-warnings).

Bring drywall flush with the finished surface of the tile, or finish exposed edges with mitered edges or Schluter strips. Set tiles into place with the palm of your hand only; rubber mallets create hairline cracks that grow over time. Use ⅛ inch tile spacers for the recommended grout joint, and ensure subfloors have fully cured for a minimum of 30 days before installation.

Always seal cement tile according to our installation instructions, whether in dry rooms or in areas exposed to moisture, such as pools, spas, and showers. Sealer selection and the two-step pre-grout and top-coat protocol are covered on the [Cement Installation Guide](https://ziatile.com/pages/cement-installation-guide). Reach out to our team at info@ziatile.com with any material or installation questions.

## Tile Usage *[Locked]*

Cement tile is suitable for interior installations and for exterior floors and walls in non-freeze/thaw climates only. The line is not suitable for outdoor floors that may receive snow or ice. Pools and spas are compatible when sealed per the installation guide. The DCOF rating is greater than 1.0; even with that traction, for wet floors and commercial wet rooms, increase grout joint size to a minimum of ⅛ inch and apply an anti-slip product after installation as standard practice.

## How It's Made *[Locked]*

Each tile is built up from the surface down. A craftsperson lays natural mineral pigments by hand into a steel pattern frame, one color at a time, mixed with cement to form a 3 mm pigmented surface layer. The frame is set into a steel mold and topped with a powdered concrete base, which becomes the body of the finished tile.

The mold is closed and the full assembly is hydraulically pressed at 3,000 lbs psi in a single stroke, which fuses the pigmented surface and the concrete body into one tile. The pressed tiles are soaked in water to begin the cure, then moved to drying racks where they cure for seven days. The tile is never fired, which is what distinguishes cement tile from ceramic.

The 3 mm pigmented surface layer is what gives cement tile its longevity and its characteristic patina. There is real color depth in the surface, not just a topcoat, which is why a cement tile floor can wear and re-seal over decades without losing its color, and why 19th-century cement floors are still in service in the European cities where the technique was first developed.
