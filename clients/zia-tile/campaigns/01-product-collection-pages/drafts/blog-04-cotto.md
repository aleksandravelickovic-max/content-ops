# The Long Life of Cotto

Cotto is unglazed terra cotta, hand-pressed and kiln-fired, a material that has shaped Tuscan farmhouses, Andalusian courtyards, and adobe interiors across the American Southwest for the better part of a thousand years. The word itself is Italian for "cooked," shorthand for clay that has been fired but never glazed. Zia's cotto line is made in San Miguel de Allende, Mexico, the same artisan town that gives [Cotto Allende](https://ziatile.com/collections/cotto-tile-allende) its name. Each tile is a record of the day it left the kiln.

## A short history of cotto

Cotto's lineage runs from the Roman Empire forward. Fired clay floors lined the villas of Pompeii and the country houses of Tuscany through the Renaissance, where the same hand-pressed terra cotta sat under the feet of merchants and stonemasons alike. Italian builders eventually adopted "cotto" as the category name for fired floor tile, distinct from majolica and the other glazed ceramics traveling out of the same kilns.

The material crossed the Atlantic with Spanish colonization in the sixteenth century. San Miguel de Allende, founded in 1542 in the central Mexican highlands, became one of several towns where European clay-firing methods met older regional pottery traditions. Artisans there adapted the form to a heavier, more saturated clay drawn from nearby beds, fired at temperatures suited to the altitude. Four centuries later, that method still shapes every tile Zia ships from San Miguel.

In the American Southwest, cotto traveled with Spanish mission architecture, from California to New Mexico. The Mediterranean Revival of the nineteen-twenties returned the material to the courtyards of Pasadena and Santa Barbara estates. Mid-century Mexican modernism then carried unglazed clay floors into contemporary interiors, where they read alongside white plaster, cantera stone, and pigment-saturated walls.

In Italy, the tradition never broke. The Milanese designer Renzo Mongiardino made cotto a signature material of his historicist interiors, specifying handmade terra cotta floors for clients including Lee Radziwill, Valentino, and the Agnelli family. Studio Peregalli, founded by his protégés Roberto Peregalli and Laura Sartori Rimini, has carried that vocabulary into contemporary Italian and Moroccan projects, where cotto remains the foundational floor.

## What is cotto tile?

Cotto is fired clay with no glaze coat. Natural clay is mixed with water to the right consistency, hand-pressed into a mold, and air-dried for one to two weeks before going into the kiln. Nothing is brushed across the face after firing. Color comes from the iron in the clay and the temperature inside the kiln, not from a finish layer. The resulting tile is matte, porous, slip-resistant (Zia's cotto carries a DCOF of 0.98), and built to develop patina with use.

Zia's [cotto collection](https://ziatile.com/collections/cotto) is offered in six core colorways: Red Clay, Fired Earth, Adobe, Blanco, Madera, and Oscura. The first three sit in the historic register of warm Mediterranean and Mexican terra cotta. Blanco is a whitewashed clay, Madera reads cocoa-brown, and Oscura is a matte black wash, three contemporary tones Zia introduced to bring cotto into rooms where the classic reds and browns would feel too literal.

Shapes follow the same logic of tradition plus expansion. The line covers standard formats (4x4, 8x8, and 13x13 squares; 2x6 and 6.5x13 rectangles; 8x9 hexagons) along with eight specialty patterns drawn from Spanish and Mexican floor traditions: Alcazar, Big Alcazar, Oaxaca, Durango, Tornillo, Zocalo, Toltec, and Stars & Cross. The shape names are not decoration. Alcazar references the geometric floors of Andalusia; Toltec points to the pre-Columbian cultures of central Mexico.

## Cotto, Saltillo, and Cotto Allende

The three names get used interchangeably online, and they should not be. Each describes a distinct product.

**Saltillo** is unglazed terra cotta produced specifically in Saltillo, Coahuila, in northern Mexico. It is often sun-dried rather than oven-dried in traditional shops, which is part of why it carries thicker edges, more irregular dimensions, and deeper color shifts. Zia does not sell Saltillo. It belongs to the broader history of Mexican terra cotta, but the cotto Zia carries is a separate product, hand-pressed in a different town and finished to a tighter dimensional standard.

**Cotto** in Zia's catalog is hand-pressed in San Miguel de Allende, Mexico, a town with its own four-century tile-making lineage. It is unglazed, matte, more dimensionally consistent than Saltillo, and rated for both interior and exterior use, including climates that freeze and thaw.

**Cotto Allende** is the glazed counterpart. It uses the same clay base and is formed by the same artisans in San Miguel de Allende. After firing, each piece receives a hand-applied glaze in matte, glossy, or smooth finish across eighteen colorways, including Arroyo, Baja, Albar, Puebla, Posada, Lupe, Dorado, Cacao, and Azul. Crazing, the fine hairline cracks that appear in the glaze during cooling, is a normal characteristic of glazed clay rather than a defect. The glaze opens Cotto Allende to applications the unglazed line does not handle as gracefully, including pool waterlines and saltwater spas, though it removes the freeze-thaw rating.

To summarize: Saltillo is a regional terra cotta Zia does not stock. Cotto is the unglazed core line, freeze-thaw rated, built to patina with use. Cotto Allende is the glazed expansion line, broader in color range, suited to wet installations the unglazed line cannot take.

## Where cotto belongs in a home

Cotto is one of the few materials Zia carries that earns a check on every line of the residential usage chart. It works for high-traffic kitchen floors, primary bathrooms, mudrooms, and entryways. It performs on covered patios and pool decks in mild climates, and equally on outdoor walkways and courtyards in cold climates that freeze and thaw. It clads fireplace surrounds, wraps range hoods, and runs up backsplashes. Walk-in showers, steam showers, and tub surrounds all sit within its range, provided the sealing protocol is followed and a wider grout joint or anti-slip topcoat is used on wet floors.

The reason cotto travels so well is that the unglazed clay body is fundamentally one material from face to back. There is no surface coat to chip, no glaze to scratch through. When the tile is sealed correctly, it shrugs off freeze-thaw cycles in a way most ceramics and many natural stones cannot.

Editorially, cotto reads warm and grounded. It belongs in rooms where the architecture is honest: exposed beams, plastered walls, hand-thrown ceramics on open shelves. Red Clay or Fired Earth pairs naturally with reclaimed oak and unlacquered brass for a Mediterranean register. Blanco run across a kitchen floor against pale plaster walls reads quieter, almost Scandinavian. Oscura behind a fireplace, when the rest of the room is light, becomes the anchor of the floor plan.

## How to care for cotto

Caring for cotto is two-part: sealing at installation, and routine cleaning afterward.

**Sealing.** Cotto leaves the kiln porous by design and must be sealed on-site. Per the [cotto installation guide](https://ziatile.com/pages/cotto-installation-guide), apply 511 Porous Plus as a pre-grout sealer in thin layers until the tile is saturated, wipe excess, and allow one to two days to cure. A top coat goes on next. For interior installations, Zia recommends Fila Matte Finish Protective Wax, applied in thin layers and cured one to two days before the room is used. For exterior installations, skip the wax (it can discolor under UV) and use 511 Porous Plus again as the top coat, curing twenty-four to forty-eight hours. Subfloors must be fully cured for at least thirty days before any tile goes down, otherwise efflorescence can deposit on the finished surface.

**Routine cleaning.** Sweep or vacuum regularly so grit does not abrade the seal. Mop with warm water and a pH-neutral cleaner such as Miracle Sealants pH Neutral Tile and Stone Cleaner. For heavier stains, Miracle Sealants Heavy Duty Cleaner (Acid Substitute) is the recommended option. Avoid acidic cleaners, vinegar, citrus formulas, and bleach products, all of which etch unglazed clay.

**Patina.** Cotto deepens and softens with use the way leather and unlacquered brass do. This is a feature of the material, not a sign of wear. Re-seal as needed based on foot traffic, especially in entryways and wet rooms.

## Choosing a cotto colorway and shape

The choice usually comes down to two questions: how much warmth does the room need, and how much pattern can it absorb.

Warmth lives on a spectrum. Red Clay is the most saturated tone in the line and reads almost lacquered when freshly sealed. Fired Earth steps the saturation back into a deeper, more grounded brick. Adobe is the muted middle, a soft tan that recedes into plaster and wood. Blanco is the cool end of the palette, useful when cotto is the floor under busy cabinetry. Madera and Oscura sit on the contemporary side, the two colorways most often specified for kitchens that want the substance of terra cotta without the historic reference.

Pattern is the other axis. A 4x4 square in Adobe across a kitchen floor reads quiet and traditional. The same colorway in Stars & Cross becomes the visual anchor of the room. Alcazar, an interlocking four-piece geometric, can lift a powder room floor without any other ornament. Hexagons, particularly in Oscura, work well as an entryway moment that signals a transition into the rest of the house.

When in doubt, order samples and lay them dry against the materials already in the room. Cotto looks markedly different under daylight, lamplight, and candlelight, and a sample test on the actual floor predicts a successful install more reliably than any moodboard.

## A long-form material

Cotto is not a fast tile. Special orders take eleven to fifteen weeks, the install requires a careful seal, and the material rewards a household willing to live with a floor that visibly ages. That is true of the other long-form materials Zia carries as well: marble, cantera, terrazzo. Each gains rather than loses with use. Cotto sits firmly in that group, the most rustic of Zia's catalog and arguably the most forgiving once it is installed. A thousand years of Italian and Mexican rooms have already proven the case.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cotto suitable for outdoor floors in cold climates?

Yes. Zia's cotto is rated for exterior floors in freeze-thaw climates when sealed correctly. For outdoor installations, use 511 Porous Plus as both the pre-grout sealer and the top coat, since wax-based finishes can discolor under direct UV.

### How often should cotto be re-sealed?

Re-seal based on traffic. Entryways, kitchens, and exterior floors typically need a fresh top coat every one to two years; low-traffic rooms can go longer. Test by dripping water on the surface: if it beads, the seal is intact; if the water darkens the tile, it is time to re-seal.

### What is the difference between cotto and Saltillo?

Saltillo is unglazed terra cotta produced in Saltillo, Coahuila, often sun-dried and noticeably less uniform in size and color. Zia's cotto is hand-pressed and kiln-fired in San Miguel de Allende, with tighter dimensional consistency and a freeze-thaw rating. Zia does not sell Saltillo.

### Can cotto be used in a shower?

Yes. Cotto is rated for walk-in showers, steam showers, and tub surrounds. A DCOF of 0.98 meets slip-resistance standards for wet floors, though Zia recommends a minimum 1/8" grout joint and an anti-slip product where the floor will be walked on barefoot.

### What is the lead time on a cotto order?

Samples ship from the Los Angeles warehouse within two business days. Special-order full-size cotto carries an eleven-to-fifteen-week lead time and cannot be cancelled once placed.

### Do the Cotto and Cotto Allende lines mix?

Yes. Both share the same clay base from San Miguel de Allende, and the dimensional formats overlap, particularly in the 4x4 square. A glazed Cotto Allende accent set against an unglazed Cotto floor is a common Zia pairing. Order samples from both lines side by side to confirm the color match before specifying.
