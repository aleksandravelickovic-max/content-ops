## About

Each cement tile carries the hand of the maker in the pigment lay, the press, and the cure: slight irregularities at the edge and small color drifts that read as a single soft matte field once the floor is laid. Color shifts subtly tile to tile within every colorway, with the deeper pigments carrying broader internal contrast than the lighter cream and bone tones.

Expect tonal variation across the box, surface character that softens with patina, and small pits or air pockets in the surface, all natural to a hand-poured pigmented cement tile. Cement tile carries a 10 to 12 week lead time on special order, so plan curation against the project schedule before placing the order.

Installers must wear protective face and eye coverings when working with cement tile, thinset, sealants, and grout, in accordance with [California Prop 65](https://www.ziatile.com/proposition-65-warnings). Bring drywall flush with the finished surface of the tile, or finish exposed edges with mitered edges or Schluter strips.

## How It's Made

Cement tile is hand-poured in Vietnam using techniques refined by French and Catalan craftspeople in the 1850s, when pigmented cement was developed as a more affordable alternative to stone mosaic. The Vietnamese workshops preserve the original method exactly: one tile at a time, by hand.

Each tile begins with mineral pigments laid by hand into a steel pattern frame, one color at a time, on top of a powdered concrete base inside a steel mold. The mold is closed, and the tile is hydraulically pressed at 3,000 lbs psi, soaked in water, and cured on drying racks for seven days. The tile is never fired, which is what distinguishes cement tile from ceramic tile.

The 3 mm pigmented surface layer is what gives cement tile its longevity and its characteristic patina. There is real color depth in the surface, not just a topcoat, which means the tile can wear and re-seal over decades without losing its color, which is part of the reason 19th-century cement tile floors are still in service.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What color is Bone Hex cement tile?

Bone Hex is a warm bone-colored cement tile — a soft, creamy neutral that sits between warm white and cream without crossing into yellow or deep tan. The bone tone carries just enough warmth to read as natural and grounded rather than stark or clinical, placing it in the clean end of the warm-neutral palette. Because the pigment in lighter colorways carries less internal contrast than deeper tones, Bone Hex reads as a consistent, softly nuanced pale field across the installed surface.

### Does Bone Hex cement tile require sealing?

Yes. Cement tile must be sealed before and after grouting. Apply 511 Porous Plus in thin layers until the tiles are fully saturated before grouting, wiping off any excess before it dries. After grouting and allowing the grout to cure fully, apply Fila Matte Finish Protective Wax as a final top coat for interior installations. For outdoor installations, apply a second coat of 511 Porous Plus in place of wax. On a pale surface, prompt sealing and regular resealing are especially important to prevent staining. Reseal every 1–2 years or as needed based on traffic and moisture exposure.

### Can Bone Hex cement tile be used in a shower?

Yes. Bone Hex cement tile can be used on shower floors and shower walls. Cement tile has a DCOF greater than 1, providing excellent slip resistance in wet conditions. The hexagonal format creates tight grout joint geometry that contributes additional texture underfoot on shower floors. Seal before and after grouting, and size grout joints to a minimum of 1/8".

### Can Bone Hex cement tile be used on an exterior floor?

Yes, in non-freeze/thaw climates. Bone Hex cement tile is suitable for exterior floors in climates that do not experience freezing temperatures. It is not rated for freeze/thaw cycling. For exterior applications, seal with 511 Porous Plus before and after grouting and use a second coat of 511 Porous Plus as the final top coat.

### Are there special installation considerations for hexagonal cement tile?

Hexagonal tiles require a layout dry-run before setting. Arrange the full field on a flat surface first to check geometry and identify any tiles with color variation you want to redistribute. The honeycomb geometry has no single dominant horizontal or vertical axis, so the starting point and orientation of the field should be planned against the room's focal wall or centerline before adhesive is applied. Blend tiles from multiple boxes throughout the layout to disperse tonal variation. Use the recommended 1/16" spacers consistently to maintain the tight, continuous grid that reads correctly at room scale.

### What pairs well with Bone Hex cement tile?

Bone Hex's warm neutral sits naturally alongside a wide range of materials without imposing temperature or direction on the space. Warm white and plaster walls extend the light, neutral palette above the floor field. Natural wood — oak, walnut, and rattan — reinforces the warm, organic character of the bone tone. Aged brass fixtures complement the warmth of the cream-white and add a warm metallic note. Deep navy, charcoal, and forest green introduced through cabinetry or textiles provide grounding contrast against the light, open field.
