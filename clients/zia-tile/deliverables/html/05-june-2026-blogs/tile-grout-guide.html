Tile Grout Guide: Choosing Grout for Zellige and Cement Tile

Tile grout decides whether a handmade installation reads as one surface or as a field of individual pieces. The joint deserves the same attention as the tile it surrounds. Our zellige, hand-shaped and glaze-dipped in Morocco, and our cement tile, pressed from mineral pigments and cement in Vietnam and never fired, each ask something different of that joint. The grout that flatters one can dull the other, so grout type, joint width, and color all follow the material.

What Tile Grout Does

Tile grout is the filler that closes the joints between set tiles, locking the field into one continuous surface. On machine-made tile it mostly disappears into thin, even lines. On handmade tile it works harder, and it shows more of itself while doing that work.

Across a zellige wall, grout flows into the chips, pits, and crazing that hand production leaves in each glazed face, blending those characteristics into the surface. The joint also carries structural weight. Our zellige installation guide calls for grout in each installation because it protects the tile edges over time, so even tiles set edge to edge receive grout worked into the natural gaps between them. Dry-stacking zellige is never an option.

Grout Types for Handmade Tile

Cement-based grout, in its sanded and unsanded forms, is the standard for handmade tile, and the split between the two follows joint width. Unsanded grout has a smooth consistency that moves into joints of 1/8 inch and narrower without scratching a glazed face. Sanded grout carries fine aggregate that gives wider joints their body and keeps them from shrinking as they cure.

Epoxy grout, a two-part resin, resists moisture and stains without sealing. It sets quickly, demands fast cleanup, and rarely earns its place in a handmade installation where the joint is meant to feel mineral.

Our installation guides name the exact grout for each material and finish, including separate specifications for glazed zellige and its unglazed natural counterpart. That specification outranks any general rule.

Grout Joints by Material

Joint width follows the tile’s edges. Zellige walls may be set edge to edge or with 1/16 inch grout joints, using wedge spacers to align the naturally irregular edges. Zellige floors call for grout joints of at least 1/8 inch in a staggered layout, which adds slip resistance underfoot.

Wet commercial floors carry a firmer specification. Grout joints there should measure at least 1/8 inch, paired with an anti-slip treatment applied after installation.

Cement tile runs narrower. Its 1/16 inch grout joints let the pattern read continuously across the floor, so a patterned motif carries from tile to tile without a visible grid interrupting it.

Grout Color: Match or Contrast

The color decision sits between grout that recedes and grout that outlines. The right approach depends on what the tile itself is doing, because each choice produces a different room.

For zellige, our installation guide recommends matching the grout closely to the tile color. A close match lets the dynamic glazes carry the room, so light moves across the sheen while chips, pits, and crazing read as texture within one surface. A contrasting joint outlines each tile instead, pulling attention from the glaze to the grid and emphasizing each irregular edge.

Cement tile reads differently. Matching the grout closely to the tile gives a more modern look, letting the joints recede so the pattern registers as a single plane. A lighter, neutral joint leans traditional and quietly frames each tile. Strong contrasting colors ask for extra care on cement, since pigment-rich grout can stain the matte, porous surface, and our guide calls for applying any contrasting grout with a grout bag instead of a float.

How to Grout Zellige

Grout for zellige follows a rhythm set by the material’s hand production. Glazed zellige soaks in room-temperature water for one to two minutes before setting, and the thin set then cures for at least 48 hours before grouting begins. Unglazed natural zellige is never soaked, and it must be sealed before grouting, with the sealer fully cured first.

The grouting itself moves in sections no larger than two feet square. A grout float presses the grout into each joint and across the surface characteristics of each face, and excess comes off immediately with a damp sponge, since dried grout is difficult to lift from a glazed surface. The grout then cures fully, typically 48 hours.

Glazed zellige needs no sealing before grouting. In wet areas, sealing follows the grout instead.

How to Grout Cement Tile

Cement tile must be sealed before grouting, in each installation. The tile is pressed, cured, and never fired, which leaves a matte, porous surface that would otherwise draw pigment straight from the grout. Only after that pre-grout sealer has cured, typically 24 to 48 hours, does grouting begin.

The work moves in small sections here as well. Our cement installation guide is direct on this point: the floor is never grouted all at once, and excess grout comes off as the work progresses, because dried grout can stain cement permanently. A rubber float presses grout into the 1/16 inch joints, and a grout bag takes over whenever a contrasting color is in play.

Cleanup stays gentle from the first pass onward. Cement tile takes pH neutral cleaners only, since acidic cleaners damage the surface.

How Sealing and Grout Work Together

Sealing order is where the two materials part ways most clearly. For glazed zellige, grout comes first. In showers, pools, and spas, a penetrating sealer then goes over the cured installation to keep continuous moisture out of the grout itself.

For cement tile and unglazed natural zellige, the order reverses. Sealer goes on first and cures completely, grout follows, and a top coat finishes the installation, with one finish suited to interiors and another to exterior work. Always seal our zellige according to our installation instructions, whether in dry spaces or in areas exposed to moisture, such as pools, spas, and showers. Cement tile follows the same principle, sealed in dry rooms and wet ones alike.

The full sequence for each material lives in our installation guides, which name the exact grout and sealer for each finish. The work itself calls for a dust mask and eye protection when handling grout, thin set, and sealants, per California Proposition 65.

Tile Grout FAQ

What type of grout works for zellige tile?

A cement-based grout suited to narrow joints and glazed surfaces works for zellige, and our installation guide specifies the exact grout for both glazed and unglazed natural zellige. The two finishes take different grouts, so the guide should be read before any material is purchased. In either case the grout must flow into 1/16 inch wall joints and settle into the chips, pits, and crazing across each face.

Can zellige be installed without grout?

No, zellige is never installed without grout. Our installation guide requires grout in all installations because it protects the tile edges over time, even when tiles are set edge to edge on a wall. The naturally irregular edges leave room that the grout fills, so a grouted edge-to-edge installation still reads as one continuous surface.

How wide should grout joints be for handmade tile?

Zellige walls take grout joints of 1/16 inch or an edge-to-edge placement, zellige floors take at least 1/8 inch, and cement tile takes 1/16 inch joints. Wet commercial floors are the strictest case, with grout joints of at least 1/8 inch plus an anti-slip treatment applied after installation. Wider joints on floors also allow a staggered layout that improves slip resistance.

What sealing does handmade tile need around grouting?

Cement tile and unglazed natural zellige are sealed before grouting and top-coated after, while glazed zellige is sealed only after grouting and only in wet areas such as showers, pools, and spas.
