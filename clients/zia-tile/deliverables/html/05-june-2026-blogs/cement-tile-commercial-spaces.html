What Cement Tile Is

Cement tile is a pressed, unfired tile made from mineral pigments and cement. Artisans pour pigment into a patterned mold by hand, back it with a cement body, and form each tile under a hydraulic press. The tile then cures without a kiln. That never-fired cure is the defining distinction from terra cotta and ceramics, and it is the reason the surface arrives matte.

Because the pigment layer is part of the tile body, pattern and color carry real depth. Each tile shows minor variation in tone and surface character. In a commercial installation of several hundred pieces, that variation is what keeps a large patterned floor reading as handmade.

Why Cement Tile Suits Commercial Spaces

Cement tile suits commercial spaces because the qualities designers want in a public room are already built in. The matte surface sits quietly under commercial lighting, with none of the glare that glossy floors throw across a dining room at midday. The pressed pigment carries pattern at architectural scale, so an 8x8 grid of our Cairo or Sonora can organize a large floor plan without signage or paint.

The material also ages well in public. Wear and time deepen the tone, and a commercial floor develops the lived-in character that hospitality designers spend budgets trying to reproduce. A cement floor set on opening day is a starting point, not a forever state, and five years of service reads as history.

Cement Tile as Restaurant Floor Tile

Restaurant floor tile has two jobs at once: it sets the room’s personality and it carries daily service. Cement tile suits both because the pattern is structural, formed in the press with the tile itself. Our 8x8 formats in patterns like our Madrid or Zig Zag can define a dining room, while a single solid colorway such as our Bone or Sage keeps an open kitchen line visually calm.

Dining rooms and pattern at scale

A patterned cement floor does the decorative work a restaurant would otherwise assign to walls and fixtures. Bold repeats such as our Monstera or Stars & Cross establish the room’s register from the entry, and quieter geometries let furniture and food lead. Designers often run pattern through the dining room and transition to a coordinating solid at service corridors, which uses the same material to mark front of house from back.

Wet zones: entries, bar areas, and restrooms

Wet commercial floors need a slip plan, and cement tile accommodates one directly. For entries, bar areas, and restrooms, grout joints should measure at least 1/8”, and an anti-slip treatment should be applied after installation. The joints add mechanical grip underfoot, while the treatment lets the matte surface stay visually quiet in rooms where water reaches the floor. Sealer Schedules and Routine Care in Commercial Settings

A written sealer schedule is the most consequential line in a commercial cement tile specification. Cement is porous and unfired, so each installation must be sealed according to our installation guides, whether the space is a dry dining room or a moisture-exposed entry. Commercial projects should also plan a resealing cycle keyed to traffic, since a hotel lobby and a boutique fitting room wear at different rates.

Routine care is a matter of consistency. Floors want a pH-neutral cleaner and a soft mop, never acidic or harsh chemical cleaners, which can dull the pigment. Walk-off mats at entries keep grit off the surface, and prompt attention to spills keeps them at the sealed surface, where they wipe away. A commercial crew briefed once on these habits can carry the routine without specialty equipment.

Maintained this way, the floor’s evolution works in the room’s favor. The tone deepens with wear, bringing beauty to the material’s aging process, and the patina becomes part of the venue’s character.

How to Specify Commercial Cement Tile

Specifying commercial cement tile comes down to a short list of decisions: pattern or solid, format by surface, a slip plan for wet zones, and a sealer and care schedule written into the operations manual. FAQ

Is cement tile suited to restaurant and commercial floors?

Cement tile is well suited to restaurant and commercial floors. Each tile is formed under a hydraulic press with mineral pigment integral to the body, so the color and pattern stay true through years of heavy foot traffic. With sealing at installation and a pH-neutral cleaning routine, a commercial cement floor serves for decades while developing the lived-in character that suits hospitality rooms.

Does commercial cement tile need to be sealed, and how often?

Yes, sealing is required for each installation, in dry spaces as well as areas exposed to moisture. Commercial floors should then be resealed on a cycle matched to traffic levels, with high-traffic dining rooms and lobbies on the shorter end. Our installation guides cover sealer selection and application, and our team can advise on a resealing schedule at info@ziatile.com or 310-844-1170.

Is cement tile slip resistant enough for wet commercial areas?

Cement tile can meet wet-area requirements when the installation is specified for them. For wet commercial floors such as entries, bar areas, and restrooms, grout joints should measure at least 1/8”, and an anti-slip treatment should be applied after installation. Together, the joints and treatment give the matte floor a wet-service plan without changing the quiet surface that makes cement useful in hospitality rooms.

How much overage should a commercial cement tile order include?

Commercial orders should include a minimum of 15% overage to account for curation and cuts. Projects that need tighter control across a large floor should plan for 20%, and those aiming for a more uniform field should plan for 25%.
