About
Each Cotto tile is made from locally-sourced clays. Our handcrafted Cotto tiles are made just outside of historic San Miguel de Allende, Mexico.


The tiles' clay formulation is mixed with water and blended to the ideal consistency, then pressed in a mold to dry for 1–2 weeks until the proper moisture level is achieved for firing. At this point, wood ash is added to the surface of our 'Fired Earth' tiles to give them their distinctive surface coloration.


Installers must wear protective face and eye coverings when working with Cotto, thinset, sealants, and grout, in accordance with California Prop 65. Bring drywall flush with the finished surface of the tile, or finish exposed edges with mitered edges or Schluter strips.

How It's Made
Cotto, derived from the Italian terra cotta or "baked earth," is part of a long lineage of fired clays that date back to the dawn of civilization itself. From Egyptian water jugs to Chinese roof tiles to Roman bricks, terra cotta has been part of the human story for as long as we've had an alphabet. The materials in Cotto tile haven't changed much over the millennia, while the firing process has evolved: from kiln furniture to mold designs to firing method, our handmade and high-fired Cotto is our modern take on an ancient tradition.


Our Cotto is produced in San Miguel de Allende, Mexico, where natural clay is pressed into molds, sun-dried, and fired in kilns by local artisans. The unglazed surface is what gives Cotto its character at installation and over time. With proper sealing, Cotto handles indoor floors, exterior floors in any climate, and freeze/thaw exposure, and the surface wears like a natural stone product as use deepens its tone.

Frequently Asked Questions
What color is Madera Cotto?
Madera is a deep cocoa terra cotta with tonal variation built into the unglazed clay body. The color comes entirely from the natural clay and the kiln-firing process, with no glaze modifying the final surface. Individual tiles carry different positions within the colorway's range, and those differences distribute across the full installation to produce a floor with natural tonal variation rather than uniform color.
What is the difference between Cotto and Cotto Allende?
Cotto is unglazed fired clay. Cotto Allende applies a glaze before firing. That difference determines where each material works: unglazed Cotto handles freeze/thaw exterior floors and develops a natural patina over time. Cotto Allende's glazed surface makes it suited to pools, spas, and saltwater or chlorine environments. Neither is interchangeable with the other for those applications.
Can Madera 8x8 Cotto be used in a kitchen?
Yes. Madera 8x8 can be used in a kitchen on both floors and walls. The tile performs well in backsplash and flooring applications when properly sealed to protect against moisture and staining. Apply 511 Porous Plus before grouting, then finish with Fila Matte Wax for interior installations. A minimum 1/8 inch grout joint is recommended for kitchen floor installations. Refer to the Cotto Installation Guide for the full sealing and installation sequence.
How do I seal Madera 8x8 Cotto?
Madera 8x8 requires 511 Porous Plus applied before grouting to saturate the surface and reduce absorption. After grouting and allowing the grout to cure, apply Fila Matte Wax as the final protective coat for interior installations. For exterior installations, apply a second coat of 511 Porous Plus in place of wax, as UV exposure causes wax to discolor over time. Periodic resealing is required to maintain resistance to moisture and staining. 
Can Madera 8x8 Cotto be used outdoors in cold climates?
Yes. The high-fired clay body tolerates freeze/thaw cycling when properly sealed. For all exterior applications, apply 511 Porous Plus before grouting and apply a second coat of 511 Porous Plus as the top coat after the grout has cured. Do not use wax outdoors. Full guidance is in the Cotto Installation Guide.
Can Madera 8x8 be mixed with other Madera Cotto formats?
Yes. Madera 8x8 can be mixed with other Madera formats in the Zia Cotto collection, including the Durango specialty shape or the Alcazar format in the same colorway. Each format is produced from the same clay body and firing process, so tiles share consistent surface character and tone across the range. Plan any mixed-format layout during the dry stage before setting begins, and open and mix tiles from all boxes to distribute tonal variation evenly.
