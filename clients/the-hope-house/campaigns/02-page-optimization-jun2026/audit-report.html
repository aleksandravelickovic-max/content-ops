Does TMS Therapy Work? What the Research Shows

TMS received FDA clearance in 2008 after a 325-patient randomized controlled trial showed a 30.6% reduction in depression scores compared to 13.2% for sham treatment. In the years since, the evidence base has grown larger and more consistent. This page covers what the clinical literature shows, who responds best, and where the research is still developing.

What the Clinical Trials Show

The 2008 pivotal trial established that active TMS produced statistically significant improvement over sham. Subsequent meta-analyses have found response rates ranging from 30% to 60% depending on patient population, prior treatment history, and the number of sessions completed. Remission rates in controlled trials hover around 30 to 33%. These figures apply to treatment-resistant depression specifically, meaning patients who had not responded to at least one adequate antidepressant trial.

The research is less consistent for depression that has not yet been designated treatment-resistant. Several trials show benefit, but effect sizes are smaller and the evidence is not yet at the same level.

How TMS Compares to Antidepressants

reframed from "No Systemic Side Effects / No Drug Interactions / No Sedation" marketing list — now mechanistic contrast grounded in how TMS differs physiologically Because TMS acts at the cortical level through targeted magnetic fields rather than through systemic circulation, its side effect profile is mechanistically different from antidepressants. Antidepressants affect serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine systems throughout the body, which is why sexual dysfunction, weight changes, and gastrointestinal effects are common. TMS stimulates a specific region of the prefrontal cortex and does not circulate through the body's chemistry at all. The published literature shows no drug interactions and no systemic side effects in the TMS arm of comparative trials. For patients whose antidepressant side effects have been a reason for discontinuing medication, this distinction is clinically relevant.

TMS also does not require sedation. Sessions are conducted in a clinical setting with the patient alert and seated. There is no recovery period and no restriction on driving or other activities afterward.

Side Effects and Risks

This typically decreases after the first few sessions as patients habituate to the sensation. Headaches in the hours following treatment are also reported, usually mild and resolving on their own.

Seizures are a rare but known risk of TMS, occurring at a rate of approximately 1 in 1,000 courses of treatment. Patients with a prior seizure history, certain medications that lower the seizure threshold, or specific implanted medical devices are screened out during the initial evaluation. The screening process is designed to identify contraindications before treatment begins.

Who Responds Best to TMS

The evidence most consistently supports TMS for major depressive disorder that has not responded to at least one antidepressant. Within that population, response rates are higher for patients who completed a full course of treatment (typically 36 sessions over six to nine weeks) and for those with shorter duration of current episode at the time of treatment. Age, gender, and specific antidepressant history have not emerged as reliable predictors.

TMS has also received FDA clearance for obsessive-compulsive disorder and for smoking cessation, with a different coil placement protocol for each. The research for these indications is less extensive than for depression but is growing.

What to Expect from a TMS Evaluation

A TMS evaluation at Scottsdale TMS Therapy begins with a review of treatment history, current medications, and any contraindications. Not every patient is a candidate. The evaluation is designed to determine whether TMS is clinically appropriate given your specific history, not to enroll everyone who inquires.

If you are a candidate, a typical course of treatment involves sessions five days per week for six to nine weeks. Most sessions last 20 to 40 minutes. The cost of a full TMS course ranges from $2,000 to $12,000 depending on the number of sessions and insurance coverage.

Frequently Asked Questions About TMS Research

Does TMS therapy actually work for depression?

Yes. TMS received FDA clearance based on clinical trial data showing statistically significant improvement over sham treatment. Meta-analyses report response rates of 30% to 60% in treatment-resistant depression populations and remission rates near 30%. It does not work for everyone, and the evidence is strongest for patients who have not responded to at least one antidepressant.

Is TMS FDA-approved?

FDA clearance (510(k) pathway) applies to devices shown to be substantially equivalent to a legally marketed predicate. FDA approval (PMA pathway) applies to high-risk devices requiring independent clinical evidence of safety and effectiveness. TMS was cleared in 2008 for major depressive disorder and has since received clearance for OCD and smoking cessation.

Does TMS have side effects?

How many sessions of TMS are needed to see results?

A standard course of TMS involves 36 sessions delivered five days per week over six to nine weeks. Some patients report improvement in the third or fourth week; others see change closer to the end of the full course. Stopping early reduces the probability of full response.

Can TMS be used alongside medication?

Yes. TMS does not interact with antidepressants or other psychiatric medications through any pharmacological mechanism. Many patients receive TMS while continuing their current medication regimen. Whether to adjust medications during TMS is a clinical decision made by the treating provider.

Opioid Withdrawal: What Happens and When

The timeline and severity of opioid withdrawal vary by the specific opioid, duration of use, and individual physiology. For short-acting opioids like heroin or immediate-release oxycodone, symptoms typically begin within 8 to 24 hours after the last dose and peak between 36 and 72 hours. For longer-acting opioids like methadone or extended-release formulations, onset may be delayed 36 to 48 hours with a longer peak.

Early symptoms include anxiety, restlessness, muscle aches, and runny nose. As withdrawal progresses, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, insomnia, and intense cravings become more prominent. The acute phase typically resolves within five to seven days, though post-acute withdrawal symptoms — including mood dysregulation and persistent cravings — can continue for weeks.

Medical Monitoring During Detox

Opioid withdrawal is rarely life-threatening in otherwise healthy adults, but the risk of relapse-and-overdose during or immediately after detox is significant. The body's tolerance resets quickly during abstinence, and a return to pre-detox use levels carries a high overdose risk. Continuous medical monitoring during detox addresses both the physiological process and this critical transition period.

The Scottsdale Detox clinical team monitors vital signs, hydration, sleep, and withdrawal severity using validated assessment tools throughout the detox process. Medication adjustments are made based on real-time clinical assessment, not a fixed schedule.

Understanding Opioid Addiction

Opioids produce their effects by binding to mu-opioid receptors in the brain, triggering the release of dopamine and producing analgesia and euphoria. With repeated use, the brain adjusts by reducing receptor sensitivity and increasing the neural signaling that counteracts opioid effects. The result is tolerance — needing more of the drug to produce the same effect — and physical dependence, in which the absence of the drug produces withdrawal.

Opioid use disorder is not the same as physical dependence. Dependence is a physiological adaptation that can occur with legitimate medical use. Opioid use disorder describes a pattern of use that continues despite harmful consequences, with loss of control over use, preoccupation with obtaining opioids, and continued use despite recognizing the harm it causes. The DSM-5 defines severity as mild (2–3 criteria), moderate (4–5 criteria), or severe (6 or more criteria).

Types of Opioids

The opioid category includes both prescription medications and illicit drugs. Prescription opioids include oxycodone, hydrocodone, codeine, morphine, hydromorphone, fentanyl (in legitimate medical formulations), and tramadol. Illicit opioids include heroin and illicitly manufactured fentanyl and its analogs. Synthetics now dominate the illicit supply and are responsible for the majority of overdose deaths.

All opioids produce withdrawal when dependence has developed and are subject to the same fundamental mechanism of opioid receptor binding. However, potency, duration of action, and onset time vary significantly — and these variables shape both the clinical picture of dependence and the detox and treatment approach.
