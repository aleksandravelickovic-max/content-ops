# Botox Consultation Guide: What to Ask Your Provider

Walking into a botox consultation when you don't know what you don't know is uncomfortable. You don't want to seem high-maintenance. You don't want to ask something "obvious." But you also have no way of knowing yet whether this particular provider is the right fit — and that's exactly what the consultation is for.

Asking thorough questions isn't difficult. It's how patients who are happy with their results got there.

---

## Why the Consultation Is the Most Important Appointment

The consultation is where you find out whether a provider has the training and judgment to treat you well — not just whether the office is clean or the receptionist is friendly.

A good consultation covers your goals, your anatomy, your medical history, the specific product and dosing being recommended, realistic expectations, and what happens if something needs to be addressed after treatment. It should feel like a conversation between two informed people, not a sales pitch.

How a provider handles the consultation tells you a lot. A rushed consultation — one where the injector jumps quickly to treatment recommendations without asking about your medical history or concerns — is worth paying attention to. So is a consultation that focuses more on add-ons than on answering your questions.

Most Botox consultations run 20 to 45 minutes. If yours is scheduled for 10 minutes, that's worth noting before you book.

## Questions to Ask About Credentials and Experience

Before anything else, you want to know who is treating you, what their training covers, and who is ultimately responsible for your care.

At a medically supervised practice, the treating injector may be a licensed cosmetic nurse injector, a physician assistant, or an aesthetic nurse practitioner — all of whom can perform Botox under physician oversight. The question to ask isn't just "are you licensed" but "who is the medical director, and what are their credentials?"

That level of physician oversight is not the norm at every medspa — it's worth asking about wherever you're considering treatment.

**Questions to bring to this part of the consultation:**

- Are you licensed to perform Botox injections in California?
- Who is the medical director at this practice?
- What are the medical director's specific credentials in facial injectables?
- What training do you have in facial anatomy and injectable placement?
- Approximately how many Botox treatments do you perform each month?

A provider who answers these questions directly and confidently is doing their job. One who deflects, minimizes, or seems put off by the question is telling you something.

## Questions to Ask About the Treatment Plan

Once you've established that the provider is qualified, the next conversation is about what, specifically, they're recommending — and why.

Botox dosing is measured in units. A qualified provider should be able to tell you how many units they're recommending, for which areas, and what product they're using. "Enough to treat your forehead" is not an answer. "20 units of Botox for the frontalis, 10 units for the glabella" is.

The neurotoxin brand also matters. Botox (onabotulinumtoxinA), Dysport (abobotulinumtoxinA), Xeomin (incobotulinumtoxinA), and Jeuveau (prabotulinumtoxinA) are all FDA-approved and work similarly, but they differ in formulation and dosing. A provider who uses one product consistently should be able to explain why.

**Ask about:**

- How many units are you recommending, and for which specific areas?
- What neurotoxin brand will you use, and what's the reason for that choice?
- Will the injection mapping be based on my specific facial anatomy and muscle movement?
- Is a two-week follow-up appointment included, or is it additional?

Vague answers on units — or a flat refusal to give a unit count — are a quality signal. Patients who don't know how many units they're getting can't evaluate whether the pricing is fair or whether the dose makes sense for their concerns.

## Questions to Ask About Realistic Results

Most patients going into their first Botox consultation have one underlying fear: looking frozen. A skilled injector addresses this without being asked.

Natural-looking results come from dosing precision and placement. Injecting fewer units in the frontalis while still addressing glabellar lines produces a softened, more mobile result than a higher-dose approach to the forehead. Your provider's dosing philosophy should match what you're describing as your goal.

**Ask:**

- Can I see before-and-after photos of patients with similar anatomy and concerns?
- How do you approach "natural-looking" results — what does that mean in terms of dosing and placement?
- What's your protocol if I'm not satisfied with the results or notice asymmetry?
- How long will the results typically last, given my treatment areas?

When you look at before-and-after photos, look for patients whose baseline looks similar to yours — similar forehead lines, similar age range. An injector who shows you only dramatic transformations without patients who match your situation may not have a large portfolio to draw from.

## Questions to Ask About Risks, Side Effects, and Complications

A trustworthy provider doesn't minimize risks. They explain them, describe how they manage them, and tell you who to call if something concerns you after treatment.

Common side effects — bruising, mild swelling, temporary headache, tenderness at injection sites — are not complications. They're expected, they're manageable, and they resolve quickly. Actual complications are rarer but worth asking about directly.

At Esthetics Center, Dr. Reginald Rice's 25+ years of experience and more than 12,000 patients treated means his team has established protocols for complications — not because problems are common, but because experienced practices prepare for them. Transparency about what can go wrong is a sign of clinical maturity, not a reason for concern.

**Ask:**

- What are the most common side effects for the areas we're treating?
- What is your protocol if I develop ptosis (eyelid drooping) or notable asymmetry?
- Who do I contact if I have a concern in the days after treatment?
- Do you have reversal agents on-site for emergency use?

A provider who answers these directly and without defensiveness is a green flag. One who tells you complications "basically never happen" without explaining their protocol is not.

## Questions to Ask About Pricing and What's Included

Botox pricing is typically structured one of two ways: by unit or by area. Unit-based pricing is more transparent. When you know the unit count and the per-unit cost, you can calculate your total and compare it fairly.

Area-based pricing isn't inherently dishonest, but it requires more follow-up questions. "Treatment for your forehead" could mean 12 units or 30, and you should know which.

**Ask:**

- Is the consultation fee applied toward my treatment if I move forward?
- Are you pricing by unit or by area, and if by area, how many units does that include?
- Is the two-week follow-up included, or does it have an additional cost?
- Do you have touch-up or satisfaction policies if the results need adjustment?

Providers who push strongly toward area-based bundles without explaining what's included in each bundle, or who apply significant pressure to add treatments during the consultation itself, are worth evaluating carefully.

## Red Flags to Watch for During Any Botox Consultation

Most of the content you'll find on this topic is written from the provider's seat: "here's what a good provider covers." This list is written from yours.

**Watch for these signals during any consultation:**

- No clear answer when you ask who the medical director is and what their credentials are
- Pressure to book a treatment or add services before your questions are answered
- No discussion of your medical history, medications, or contraindications
- Vague or evasive answers when you ask about unit counts or which product is being used
- No mention of a follow-up appointment as a standard part of the treatment
- A "consultation" conducted entirely over text message, DM, or social media with no in-person assessment
- Dismissiveness when you ask about risks or complications

You are evaluating them as much as they are evaluating your candidacy for treatment. A qualified provider understands this and welcomes the conversation.

| Green Flag | Red Flag |
|---|---|
| Asks about your medical history and medications | Skips medical history entirely |
| States unit count and product name clearly | Vague on units; won't name the product |
| Medical director's credentials are clearly stated | Can't or won't answer who the medical director is |
| Follow-up appointment is standard and included | No mention of follow-up |
| Directly explains risks and their management protocol | "Complications basically never happen here" |
| Takes your time to answer all your questions | Rushes toward booking before you've finished asking |
| Addresses the "frozen look" concern proactively | Dismisses the concern without explaining dosing philosophy |

## How to Prepare Before Your Consultation

The consultation goes better when you've done a bit of preparation. Here's what to bring and what to start ahead of time.

**What to bring:**

- A list of all current medications and supplements — particularly blood thinners, NSAIDs (ibuprofen, aspirin, naproxen), vitamin E, and fish oil
- Photos of the expressions that bother you most (frowning, raising your brows, squinting)
- Photos of your neutral face in good lighting
- Reference photos if you have them — but frame them as directional, not as a guarantee
- Any questions you've written down ahead of time

**What to stop before treatment (if your doctor approves):**

- Blood-thinning supplements: stop 7–10 days before your appointment
- NSAIDs: stop 24–48 hours before treatment
- Alcohol: typically 24 hours before

After the consultation, if you decide to move forward, your provider will give you pre-treatment instructions for the day of your appointment — what to avoid, how to come in, and what to expect. Ask about those during the consultation so you have time to plan.
