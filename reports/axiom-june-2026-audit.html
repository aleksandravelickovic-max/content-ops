HRMS FAQs: How to Choose, Implement, and Secure Your HR System

What is an internal HR system?

An internal HR system is software that centralizes payroll, benefits, compliance, time tracking, and employee data in one platform instead of separate tools. Axiom implements UKG Ready, one of the top-rated HRMS platforms for mid-market businesses, and handles the full setup so your team doesn’t start from scratch.

What is the difference between an HRMS and an HRIS?

An HRIS stores and manages employee data, covering records, onboarding, and reporting. An HRMS goes further, adding payroll, workforce management, and automation on top of that foundation. Most modern platforms, including UKG Ready, cover both functions in one system, so the distinction matters less than whether the platform handles your full set of requirements.

How do I choose HR software for my business?

Look at what’s actually failing in your current setup: payroll errors, compliance gaps, too many disconnected tools. Your HR software should solve those specific problems without adding more complexity. Axiom helps businesses with 50 to 2,000 employees identify the right fit before picking anything.

What should I look for in an HRMS implementation partner?

Look for a partner that handles data migration, configuration, and training, then stays with you after go-live. A good HRMS implementation partner knows the platform well enough to configure it around your business, not just turn it on. Axiom’s team handles the full implementation and remains your point of contact long-term.

What does HR system implementation look like with Axiom?

Axiom handles the full process: needs assessment, data migration, configuration, user training, and support after go-live. You work with the same team through every phase, so the people who configured the system understand your setup when something comes up later.

Can Axiom’s platform scale as my business grows?

Yes. UKG Ready is built for mid-market organizations and handles more workforce complexity as your headcount grows. Axiom adjusts your configuration, support model, and platform tier as your needs change, so you don’t have to replace the system when you add more employees.

Is an internal HR system a cloud-based solution?

Yes. UKG Ready runs in the cloud, so your team can access it from any location without on-premise infrastructure. Updates roll out automatically, and your data stays encrypted and backed up on UKG’s enterprise infrastructure.

How does an internal HR system handle compliance?

The system tracks labor law changes, manages documentation, and flags issues automatically. Axiom’s team also monitors regulatory updates that affect your industry and keeps your configuration current, so you don’t have to manually track every change in employment law.

Why work with Axiom instead of going directly to UKG?

With UKG direct, you get the software. With Axiom, you get a team that knows your account, handles implementation, and is available after go-live. Axiom is a UKG Ready Preferred Partner, which means more implementation depth and dedicated support than you get through a standard vendor relationship.

When to Outsource HR: The Signals That Tell You It's Time

Payroll lands on the owner's desk. Benefits renewal comes up, and nobody owns it. A new hire asks about FMLA eligibility, and the answer takes three days to find. These incidents signal that your HR has grown past the person managing it.

Companies that outsource HR usually reach that decision when something breaks, or nearly breaks, and it forces the question. Most often, that moment comes around 50 employees, when federal obligations like FMLA and ACA activate, or when the business expands into a second state and suddenly has a different set of payroll tax and leave laws to manage.

This guide covers the most common signals for when to outsource HR functions, the types of companies that typically make the move, and what to consider before you decide.

Key takeaways

  • Companies most commonly outsource HR when administrative complexity has outpaced internal capacity, typically around 50 employees, or when expanding across state lines.
  • Payroll processing, benefits administration, and compliance monitoring are the 3 most commonly outsourced HR functions because they are deadline-driven and legally consequential.
  • For a 75-person company, outsourcing HR often costs less than a single full-time HR hire once you factor in salary, benefits, and software.
  • Mid-market companies in healthcare, manufacturing, and construction most frequently outsource HR due to high compliance exposure and complex workforce structures.
  • You can outsource select HR functions without handing off your entire HR operation.
  • Outsourcing HR is a poor fit when a company already has sufficient internal HR capacity or is still early in a recent platform migration.

Signs It's Time to Outsource HR

The clearest sign is that HR tasks are being handled by people whose job is not HR. Beyond that, four operational triggers reliably indicate a company has reached the point where outsourcing makes more sense than staying in-house.

1. HR Tasks Are Landing on the Wrong People's Desks

When payroll runs through an operations manager and onboarding paperwork falls to whoever has a spare hour, the problem is structural, not a staffing gap. The company has grown past the "everyone pitches in" model, but hasn't built the function to replace it. Time that should go toward management, operations, or growth gets absorbed by administrative work that requires specialists to do correctly.

2. You've Had Payroll Errors, Late Filings, or Compliance Gaps

A missed tax deadline, a misclassified employee, or an I-9 with documentation errors is not a one-off mistake. They're signals that the volume or complexity has exceeded what the current setup can reliably handle. The HR outsourcing risks companies worry about are actually a more accurate description of what happens when compliance work is left with someone not trained for it.

3. Headcount Is Approaching or Has Crossed 50

At 50 employees, FMLA eligibility requirements activate. Under the ACA, employer coverage mandates apply at 50 full-time equivalents. These aren't soft thresholds. They carry penalties. Most companies crossing that line don't realize how much the HR footprint needs to change until something surfaces during an audit or a leave request creates legal exposure.

4. Your Workforce Has Crossed State Lines

Each state adds its own payroll tax structure, leave laws, and reporting requirements. Indiana and California handle overtime differently. Some states require paid sick leave; others don't. Managing multi-state payroll without a system built for it is one of the most common reasons mid-market companies decide the current setup can't continue.

What Types of Companies Outsource HR

Time and Labor Management: What It Is and Why It Matters

Every pay period, someone on your HR or payroll team cross-checks timesheets, corrects missed punches, and manually recalculates overtime for employees who worked across two states. The process takes hours. It repeats every two weeks. And it is exactly where payroll errors, compliance gaps, and labor cost overruns quietly accumulate.

Time and labor management is the system that replaces this. It covers how your employees record hours, how those hours connect to pay rules, how schedules are built and maintained, how leave is administered, and how all of it flows into payroll and compliance reporting.

This guide explains what a TLM system includes, why it matters most for hourly workforces, and what to look for when choosing a solution.

What Time and Labor Management Covers

Time and labor management (TLM) is the set of processes and systems your organization uses to track time worked, manage employee schedules, administer leave, and enforce wage-and-hour compliance. It sits within the broader category of workforce management systems but has a defined scope: connecting worked hours to pay calculations, compliance rules, and labor cost visibility.

It is worth knowing how TLM differs from time and attendance (T&A). A T&A system records when your employees start and stop work. A TLM system does that, but also applies pay rules to the data, then connects that data to payroll, generates compliance reports, and informs scheduling decisions. T&A is a component of TLM, not a synonym for it.

The Core Components of a TLM System

A complete time and labor management system spans five functional areas. All five share the same underlying data in a well-built platform.

Time Tracking

Time tracking is the process by which hours enter the system. Methods include biometric time clocks, web-based punch-in, mobile apps with GPS verification, and geofencing for field-based teams. What matters most is that the data flows directly into pay calculations without manual re-entry.

Attendance and Absence Management

Attendance management covers monitoring patterns, processing leave requests, and running accrual calculations for PTO, sick time, and other leave types. Accrual rules vary by state, and some states layer their own family leave requirements on top of FMLA. A TLM system automatically applies these rules against each employee's location and classification.

Employee Scheduling

Scheduling connects labor demand to shift coverage. A TLM system links the schedule to real-time data so managers can see coverage gaps before a shift starts. Employees submit shift-swap requests and view their schedules through a self-service portal.

Payroll Integration

Hours recorded in TLM need to be accurately reflected in payroll. In a single-database platform, time data and payroll rules live in the same system. Overtime calculates at the correct rate, shift differentials apply automatically, and the pay run draws from the same records your HR and managers have reviewed during the period.

When TLM and payroll run on separate systems connected via data exports, each transfer point is a potential source of record discrepancies. Axiom's payroll services operate from the same database as the time and labor module, removing that reconciliation step entirely.

Compliance Tracking and Reporting

A TLM system maintains a timestamped record of every clock event, schedule change, and manager approval. Those records support audits, respond to wage disputes, and satisfy record-keeping requirements under state and federal law.

Why TLM Matters More for Hourly Workforces

Time and attendance tracking for hourly employees means recording and verifying every worked hour at the minute level, then applying the correct pay rate to each category of time: regular, overtime, holiday, and shift differential. Because hourly employees are non-exempt under the FLSA by default, every hour they work has a direct wage consequence.

If your system misses an overtime threshold, you are out of compliance with federal law, not just with payroll policy.

Hourly workers also face a higher risk of time theft. Buddy punching and manual timesheet inflation are most common in environments where oversight is limited. The cost accumulates quietly.

How TLM Keeps You on the Right Side of Wage and Hour Law

A TLM system handles overtime by applying the applicable pay rule automatically at the point of calculation. Under the FLSA, your non-exempt employees must receive 1.5 times their regular rate for any hours worked beyond 40 in a workweek.

Federal overtime is the floor. California and Alaska require daily overtime: 1.5x after 8 hours in a day, and double time after 12 hours in California. Several other states have their own break and rest-period requirements.

Predictive scheduling laws add another layer. Cities including Chicago, New York, San Francisco, and Seattle require employers to post schedules in advance, typically 10 to 14 days, and to pay a premium when schedules change on short notice.

The FLSA requires payroll records to be retained for at least 2 years; some state laws extend that period to 3 years. Your TLM system maintains those records automatically.

Time and Labor Management: What It Is and Why It Matters

Time and labor management is the system that replaces the manual work that happens every pay period: cross-checking timesheets, correcting missed punches, recalculating overtime for employees who worked across two states. That process takes hours, repeats every two weeks, and is where payroll errors, compliance gaps, and labor cost overruns quietly accumulate.

This guide explains what a TLM system includes, why it matters most for hourly workforces, and what to look for when choosing a solution.

What Time and Labor Management Covers

Time and labor management (TLM) is the set of processes and systems your organization uses to track time worked, manage employee schedules, administer leave, and enforce wage-and-hour compliance. It connects worked hours to pay calculations, compliance rules, and labor cost visibility.

A T&A system records when employees start and stop work. A TLM system does that, plus applies pay rules, connects to payroll, generates compliance reports, and informs scheduling. T&A is a component of TLM, not a synonym.

The Core Components of a TLM System

Time Tracking

Methods include biometric time clocks, web-based punch-in, mobile apps with GPS verification, and geofencing. The data flows directly into pay calculations without manual re-entry.

Attendance and Absence Management

Covers monitoring patterns, leave requests, and accrual calculations for PTO, sick time, and other leave types — applied automatically against each employee's location and classification.

Employee Scheduling

Links the schedule to real-time data so managers see coverage gaps before a shift starts. Employees submit shift-swap requests and view schedules through a self-service portal.

Payroll Integration

In a single-database platform, time data and payroll rules live in the same system — overtime calculates at the correct rate, shift differentials apply automatically. Axiom's payroll services operate from the same database as the time and labor module, removing the reconciliation step entirely. Axiom's team provides hands-on support through implementation and ongoing operations.

Compliance Tracking and Reporting

A TLM system maintains a timestamped record of every clock event, schedule change, and manager approval — supporting audits and wage disputes automatically.

Why TLM Matters More for Hourly Workforces

Because hourly employees are non-exempt under the FLSA by default, every hour they work has a direct wage consequence. If your system misses an overtime threshold, you are out of compliance with federal law — not just payroll policy.

How TLM Keeps You on the Right Side of Wage and Hour Law

Under the FLSA, non-exempt employees must receive 1.5x their regular rate for hours beyond 40 per workweek. California and Alaska require daily overtime: 1.5x after 8 hours, double time after 12 in California. Predictive scheduling laws in Chicago, New York, San Francisco, and Seattle require 10–14 days advance notice. The FLSA requires payroll records retained for at least 2 years.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between TLM and time and attendance?

T&A records start/stop times. TLM adds pay rule application, overtime calculation, leave accrual tracking, scheduling, and compliance reporting. T&A is one component within a TLM system.

How does TLM software calculate overtime automatically?

The system tracks accumulated hours in real time. When hours cross the FLSA threshold, the 1.5x rate applies automatically. For states with daily overtime rules, the calculation runs against daily totals.

Can TLM software handle multi-state compliance?

Yes — if configured to apply state-specific pay rules, break requirements, and overtime thresholds by employee location. Multi-state compliance is one of the most common failure points for organizations that have grown beyond a single-state setup.

HRO vs. PEO vs. ASO: What's the Difference?

HRO, PEO, and ASO are three different outsourcing structures with fundamentally different legal and operational implications. Which one fits your company depends on how much control you want to retain and what you need the provider to carry.

HRO (Human Resource Outsourcing)

The provider delivers HR services: payroll, compliance, benefits administration, and recruiting support. You remain the employer of record. All employment decisions stay with your company.

PEO (Professional Employer Organization)

The PEO enters a co-employment arrangement. It becomes a joint employer of record for tax and benefits purposes, allowing it to pool employees from multiple client companies to offer better group rates. In exchange, you share some control over employment-related decisions.

ASO (Administrative Services Only)

The provider handles administrative processing (payroll, tax filings, and some HR admin) without co-employment or strategic HR support. It is a narrower version of HRO, suited to companies with internal HR leadership that want to offload transaction processing.

Benefits of Outsourcing HR

The core business case for outsourcing HR is cost structure and expertise. You trade a fixed internal HR cost for a variable per-employee fee, and you gain access to specialists your company's size typically cannot afford to hire full-time.

  • Lower total cost: A full-time HR hire carries base salary, benefits, payroll taxes, software licenses, and overhead. Per-employee outsourcing fees for the same scope of work often run lower when those total costs are added up.
  • Compliance coverage across every jurisdiction you operate in: FLSA overtime thresholds, ACA reporting requirements, and state-by-state leave laws all require ongoing monitoring. The provider tracks that; your team does not have to.
  • Time redirected from administration to strategy: When your HR lead stops processing payroll manually and fielding benefits enrollment calls, that capacity moves toward hiring, culture, and retention work.
  • Access to enterprise-level HR technology: Most HRO providers include a unified HCM platform as part of the service — scheduling, self-service, analytics, and payroll in a single system — without requiring a separate software purchase.
  • Scalability without proportional headcount growth: Adding 30 employees does not require adding HR staff when the provider scales with you.
  • Competitive benefits access: Providers working with multiple clients can offer group rates for health, dental, and vision coverage that a standalone company of your size may not be able to negotiate on its own.

Risks and Tradeoffs to Understand Before You Outsource

Loss of Responsiveness

A provider serves multiple clients. Your employees may wait longer for answers to benefits or payroll questions than they would with an internal contact who sits down the hall. A named account manager — one person who knows your company specifically — is the right ask during contract negotiations, not a concession to make later.

Cultural Misalignment

A provider managing employee relations does not know your business the way an internal hire does. If they handle a sensitive situation generically, it costs you trust with employees, not just efficiency. Before signing, ask specifically how they handle employee-facing communication and escalations.

Data Security

Employee records, payroll data, and benefits information will live on the provider's systems. Ask about encryption standards, access controls, breach notification procedures, and what happens to your data when the contract ends. Get those answers in writing.

Scope Creep

Initial pricing looks clean. Six months in, requests outside the original scope start generating add-on fees. Define the scope of work at the function level, not just the category level, and agree on how out-of-scope work is handled and priced before you sign.

Knowledge Loss During Transitions

If a provider relationship ends, your institutional HR knowledge may be sitting in their systems. Get contractual clarity on data portability and the exit process upfront.

What the Transition Looks Like

Step 1: HR Audit

Before onboarding a provider, inventory your current state: which HR functions you are running, where data lives, what contracts and benefits are in place, which compliance filings are open or upcoming, and what employee documentation needs to be transferred. Step 2: Vendor Onboarding

This phase covers data migration (employee records, payroll history, benefits enrollment data), system configuration, policy alignment, and employee communication. Your employees need to know who handles their HR questions now, how to access the new system, and what, if anything, changes in their day-to-day experience.

Step 3: Go-Live and Stabilization

Most payroll implementations include a parallel processing period: running payroll through both the old and new systems for one or two cycles to verify accuracy before fully cutting over. Plan for a stabilization window of four to eight weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions About HR Outsourcing

Is HR outsourcing right for small businesses?

Yes, depending on which functions you outsource. Businesses with fewer than 25 employees typically benefit most from payroll outsourcing alone. Full HRO is usually more than they need at that size. Once headcount reaches 50 or more, especially with multi-state operations or complex benefits requirements, full HR outsourcing generally delivers a cost and compliance advantage over a solo in-house HR hire.

Can you outsource HR if you already have an internal HR team?

Yes. Most mid-market companies that outsource HR keep at least one internal HR person. That person shifts from processing and administration to strategy, culture, and employee relations — all work that requires institutional context a provider cannot carry.

What is the difference between HR outsourcing and HR consulting?

HR consulting is project-based. A consultant comes in to solve a specific problem and then the engagement ends. HR outsourcing is ongoing. The provider runs designated HR functions as a continuous service, not as a one-time project.

How is HR outsourcing different from buying HR software?

HR software gives your team tools to manage HR functions more efficiently. HR outsourcing transfers the execution of those functions to a provider. If your HR team is overwhelmed by volume, adding software does not reduce it. Outsourcing does.

What happens if you need to exit an HR outsourcing contract?

Standard contract exit terms include a notice period (typically 30–90 days), data export in a portable format, and a final accounting of outstanding fees. Get those terms in writing before you sign, and confirm that your employee data can be exported in a format your next provider or internal team can use from day one.
