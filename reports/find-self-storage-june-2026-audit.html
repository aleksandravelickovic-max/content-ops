Car Storage Units: Find Indoor, Covered & Outdoor Car Storage Near You

Whether you're reclaiming your garage from a seasonal sports car, protecting a classic during winter, or need long-term secure parking while you travel, car storage units offer a safer and more accessible alternative to leaving your vehicle exposed on a driveway or crammed into a shared space.

Who Should Use Car Storage?

Car and vehicle storage fits a wide range of situations:

  • Seasonal drivers: Classic cars, convertibles, and sports cars stored during winter months
  • Collectors and enthusiasts: Multiple vehicles with insufficient home garage space
  • Military members and frequent travelers: Long-term, secure parking while away for extended periods
  • Snowbirds: Leaving a vehicle behind when splitting time between residences
  • Homeowners in transition: Garage under renovation, downsizing, or converting to another use
  • Owners of oversized vehicles: Full-size trucks, vans, or vehicles banned from residential parking

Types of Car Storage Units

The three main types of car storage offer different levels of protection at different price points. Your choice should match your vehicle's value, storage duration, and local climate.

Enclosed (Indoor) Car Storage

Fully enclosed units work like a private garage. The vehicle sits inside a sealed drive-up unit with a roll-up door, protected from rain, hail, UV rays, dust, rodents, and temperature extremes. This is the highest-protection option and the right choice for anything valuable or stored long-term.

Best for: Classic and collector cars, luxury vehicles, winter storage in cold climates, and any vehicle where paint, interior, or mechanical condition matters.

Unit SizeDimensionsFits

Sizing note: If your vehicle has a lift kit, roof rack, or oversize tires, measure actual vehicle height and compare against both the unit's interior clearance and door opening height, as these are often different measurements.

Covered Car Storage

Covered storage parks your vehicle under a standalone canopy roof with open sides. You get overhead protection from rain, hail, and direct sun without the fully enclosed unit cost.

Best for: Daily drivers or lightly used vehicles where budget matters more than maximum protection.

Limitations: Open sides mean limited protection from rodents, temperature extremes, wind-driven rain, or flooding. Typically secured with perimeter fencing and gated entry.

Outdoor Car Storage

A secured, open parking lot with perimeter fencing and surveillance. No weather protection, but access is easy and pricing is the lowest of the three options.

Best for: RVs, trailers, and vehicles built for outdoor exposure. Also practical for short-term storage in mild climates.

Should You Look for Climate Controlled Car Storage?

Climate controlled car storage (also called temperature-controlled or heated/cooled storage) keeps the interior of the unit at a stable temperature year-round, typically between 55 and 85 degrees Fahrenheit.

Not all facilities offer this for vehicles, and availability varies by location. When searching for car storage, check individual facility listings to see whether this option is available near you.

It is worth seeking out if any of the following apply:

  • You're in a region with extreme heat, hard freezes, or high year-round humidity
  • Storage duration is 3 months or longer
  • The vehicle has leather upholstery, sensitive electronics, or significant collector value
  • You're concerned about battery discharge in freezing temperatures or fluid thickening in extreme cold

What climate control protects against:

  • Paint oxidation and cracking from heat spikes
  • Rubber seal deterioration from UV and temperature cycling
  • Mold and condensation buildup from humidity
  • Battery failure in freezing conditions
  • Fluid thickening (oil, brake fluid, coolant) in extreme cold

For a modern daily driver stored short-term in a mild climate, covered or standard enclosed storage is usually adequate. For anything valuable, long-term, or in a harsh climate, climate control is worth checking availability for when you compare facilities.

Car Storage Security Features to Look For

When comparing facilities, look for these security features before committing to a unit:

  • Perimeter fencing with controlled gate access (PIN pad or key fob)
  • 24/7 video surveillance with recorded footage
  • Individual unit alarms on enclosed units
  • On-site management or regular staffed hours
  • Well-lit access areas throughout the property
  • Drive-up unit access to protect your vehicle during loading

Ask facilities specifically about overnight, weekend, and holiday security protocols, not just what is listed on the website.

How to Prepare Your Car for Long-Term Storage

Stored cars develop predictable problems — dead batteries, flat-spotted tires, and degraded fuel that gums up injectors and carburetors. Taking a few hours before storage prevents most of them.

Change the oil and oil filter: Used engine oil contains acidic byproducts that cause internal corrosion when left sitting. Change the oil immediately before storage and do not defer to the next regular interval.

Drain and replace the cooling system: Use a 50/50 antifreeze-to-water mix rated for your climate's lowest expected temperature. During summer months, it's common to run on straight water, so switch to the proper mix before winter storage.

Top off transmission, steering, and differential fluids: Full fluid reservoirs reduce oxidation and confirm proper lubrication when the vehicle is restarted after a long idle period. Always use the fluid types specified in your owner's manual.

Check and top off brake fluid: A dry brake system is a safety hazard. Top off the reservoir before storage and inspect for any existing leaks.

Fill the fuel tank and add fuel stabilizer: A full tank prevents moisture and condensation inside the fuel system. Add a fuel stabilizer (such as Sta-Bil) for any storage period over 30 days to prevent fuel degradation and varnish buildup in injectors and carburetors.

Inflate tires to the correct PSI: Tires develop flat spots under prolonged static load. Inflate to the vehicle manufacturer's specification, or slightly above. For storage exceeding six months, consider tire cradles or flat-spot prevention stands.

Wash, wax, and cover the exterior: Dirt and contaminants accelerate paint oxidation. Wash the vehicle, apply a coat of wax, and treat rubber trim with a UV protectant. Inside an enclosed unit, use a breathable fabric car cover. Avoid non-breathable plastic covers, which trap condensation against the paint.

Clean the interior thoroughly: Vacuum the carpets and seats, wipe down all surfaces, and remove all food, beverages, and organic materials. Organic matter attracts pests and causes mold and odor during storage.

Disconnect the battery or connect a maintainer: Disconnect the negative terminal to prevent phantom drain from the clock, alarm system, and onboard electronics. For long-term storage, a battery maintainer (trickle charger) is the better option, as it keeps the battery at proper charge without overcharging. Clean terminal connectors with a baking soda and water solution before reinstalling.

Seal the exhaust and air intake: Use a clean rag, foam plug, or plastic bag (secured with a rubber band) to cover the exhaust tip and air intake opening. This prevents rodents and insects from nesting inside. Mark or tag these covers clearly so you do not start the vehicle without removing them first.

Run the vehicle periodically: If your facility permits it, return every two weeks to start the engine and run it for at least 15 to 20 minutes. Drive slowly within the facility if possible to prevent tire flat-spotting and keep all lubricated components in good condition. Reconnect the battery and remove exhaust plugs each time before starting.

Car Storage FAQs

How much does it cost to store a car per month?
Expect to pay $25 to $75 per month for outdoor lot storage, $50 to $150 for covered parking, and $75 to $400 or more for enclosed units depending on size and whether climate control is available. Prices vary significantly by location, with urban markets running higher than suburban or rural areas.
What size storage unit do I need for a car?
A 10' x 15' unit fits most compact cars and sports cars. A 10' x 20' accommodates sedans, crossovers, and minivans. Full-size pickups and vans typically require a 10' x 25' or 10' x 30' unit. Always measure your vehicle's height and verify it clears the facility's door opening before renting.
Can I store a car in a regular self storage unit?
Yes. Many standard drive-up self storage units are large enough for a vehicle. You'll need at least a 10' x 15' for compact cars and a 10' x 20' or larger for full-size vehicles. Confirm with the facility that vehicle storage is permitted and that door clearance matches your car's height.
Is climate controlled storage necessary for a car?
It depends on the vehicle and the storage duration. Climate control is worth seeking for classic or collector cars, storage of 3 or more months, and regions with extreme temperatures or high humidity. It protects paint, rubber seals, electronics, and fluids from damage. Availability varies by facility, so check listings near you to see which locations offer this option.
What is the difference between covered and enclosed car storage?
Enclosed storage places your car inside a fully sealed drive-up unit with a roll-up door. It provides complete protection from weather, pests, dust, and temperature extremes. Covered storage parks your vehicle under a standalone roof with open sides. It protects from direct rain and sun but not from rodents, flooding, or temperature extremes.
How do I prepare my car for long-term storage?
Key steps: change the oil, fill the tank and add fuel stabilizer, top off all fluids, inflate tires, disconnect the battery or connect a maintainer, wash and wax the exterior, clean the interior, and plug the exhaust and air intake. Run the engine for 15 to 20 minutes every two weeks when possible.

Dock fees add up fast, and most HOAs prohibit boats and trailers on residential lots outright. When the season ends and haul-out is due, finding a dedicated storage spot before the rush is the practical move. Search facilities listed on FindSelfStorage.com to compare enclosed, covered, outdoor, and dry stack options by size and price — reserve online for free, no credit card required.

Who Needs Boat Storage?

Boat storage is a practical solution for a wide range of watercraft owners:

  • Seasonal boaters: sailboats, fishing boats, and ski boats stored during winter months
  • Owners without driveway space: boats and trailers that are too large to park at home
  • HOA-restricted homeowners: communities that prohibit boat and trailer parking on residential lots
  • Saltwater boaters: boats that benefit from enclosed protection between outings to prevent corrosion
  • Cabin cruiser and pontoon owners: larger vessels that require dedicated sized spaces
  • Jet ski and PWC owners: personal watercraft stored away from direct weather exposure

Types of Boat Storage Units

Boat storage comes in four main configurations. The right choice depends on your boat's size, value, how often you use it, and your budget.

Enclosed (Indoor) Boat Storage

Enclosed boat storage places your vessel inside a sealed drive-up unit with a roll-up door, similar to a private garage. It provides the highest level of protection from weather, UV damage, dust, and pests.

Best for: Boats stored for a full season, high-value watercraft, saltwater boats that benefit from drying out in a sealed environment, and boats with sensitive electronics or upholstery.

To use an enclosed unit, your boat and trailer must fit within the unit dimensions. Measure your boat's overall length on the trailer (including the hitch tongue), width, and height (including the motor if tilted) before comparing unit sizes.

Covered Boat Storage

Covered storage parks your boat under a roof structure with open sides. You get protection from rain, hail, and direct sun without the cost of a fully enclosed unit.

Best for: Boaters who use their vessel regularly and want some weather protection between outings without paying for full enclosure. A good middle-ground option for mild climates.

Limitations: Limited protection from wind-driven rain, humidity, rodents, or temperature extremes. Most covered facilities use perimeter fencing and gated access for security.

Outdoor (Open) Boat Storage

Outdoor storage is a secured, open-air lot with fencing and surveillance. No weather protection, but pricing is the most affordable option and access is typically flexible.

Best for: Boat owners on a tight budget, boats already designed for outdoor exposure, or short-term storage in mild-weather climates where precipitation is low.

If choosing outdoor storage, a quality boat cover is a worthwhile investment to protect the hull, cockpit, and motor from UV rays and moisture between uses.

Dry Stack Boat Storage

Dry stack (also called stack storage or rack storage) is a specialized system where a marina or facility uses large forklift-style equipment to store boats on steel racks, stacked several units high inside a large covered or enclosed building. When you want to use your boat, staff retrieve it with the forklift and launch it for you.

Best for: Smaller to mid-size powerboats in coastal or lakefront markets. Ideal for boats stored for weeks or months at a time. Not practical for boaters who want spontaneous, self-service access.

Advantages: Excellent protection, efficient use of space, and many dry stack facilities are waterfront-adjacent, making launch and retrieval easy.

Limitations: Access requires advance notice and is dependent on facility staffing hours. Not suitable for boats over a certain size or weight. Less common inland.

Should You Look for Climate Controlled Boat Storage?

Climate controlled boat storage maintains a stable interior temperature year-round, typically between 55 and 85 degrees Fahrenheit. Availability varies by facility, so check individual listings near you to see which locations offer this option.

It is worth seeking out if any of the following apply:

  • You're storing a boat with a fiberglass hull in a region with extreme temperature swings
  • The vessel has leather upholstery, wood trim, or high-end electronics
  • You're in a high-humidity coastal environment where mold and mildew are a concern
  • Storage duration is 3 months or longer

How to Prepare Your Boat for Storage

Skipping prep costs more than the storage unit itself. Corrosion works through cooling passages over a single winter, osmotic blistering can compromise a fiberglass hull after one freeze/thaw cycle, and mold in upholstery and canvas rarely comes out cleanly.

Flush the engine with fresh water: Particularly critical for saltwater boats. Connect a flushing attachment to the lower unit and run the engine on fresh water for at least 10 minutes. This clears salt, sand, and debris from the cooling passages before they can cause corrosion.

Change the engine oil and filter: Old oil contains acids and combustion byproducts that corrode engine internals during long idle periods. Change the oil and filter immediately before storage, regardless of where you are in your normal service interval.

Fog the engine cylinders: With the engine running, spray fogging oil into the carburetor or throttle body. This coats cylinder walls with a protective film that prevents rust during storage. Shut the engine off while fogging so the oil remains on the cylinder walls.

Stabilize the fuel: Fill the tank to at least 95% capacity to minimize condensation buildup. Add a marine-grade fuel stabilizer (such as Sta-Bil Marine) and run the engine for 10 minutes to circulate it through the entire fuel system. This prevents varnish deposits in carburetors and injectors.

Flush and service the cooling system: Drain all water from the engine block, water pump, and any water lines. In cold climates, use marine antifreeze (propylene glycol) in the cooling system to prevent freeze damage. Do not skip this step if temperatures in your area drop below freezing.

Disconnect or maintain the battery: Disconnect the negative terminal to prevent phantom drain. For storage over 30 days, connect a marine battery maintainer to keep the battery at full charge without overcharging. Remove the battery entirely if storing outdoors or in an unheated space.

Clean the hull and apply protective wax: Wash the hull thoroughly, removing all algae, barnacles (if saltwater), waterline stains, and grime. Apply a coat of marine wax to the above-waterline gelcoat to protect the finish during storage. Do not leave bottom paint exposed to air longer than necessary before re-launching.

Clean and protect the interior: Remove all seat cushions, canvas covers, carpeting, and any porous materials that can trap moisture and grow mold. Wipe down all surfaces, treat vinyl upholstery with a UV protectant, and leave compartment hatches slightly open to allow airflow.

Remove all electronics and valuables: Remove fish finders, GPS units, stereo heads, and any portable electronics. Store these separately in a climate-controlled environment. This also reduces theft risk while the boat is in storage.

Lubricate all fittings and moving parts: Grease the trailer wheel bearings, lube the steering cable, and apply corrosion inhibitor to all metal fittings, hinges, latches, and clamps. This prevents seizing during the storage period.

Cover the boat properly: Use a breathable, boat-specific cover sized for your vessel. Secure it tightly to prevent wind from lifting it. Avoid non-breathable plastic tarps, which trap condensation and accelerate mold growth. In enclosed storage, a well-fitted cover still protects against dust.

Check and inflate trailer tires: Trailer tires are prone to flat-spotting and UV cracking. Inflate to the maximum recommended PSI and park the trailer on blocks or stands to relieve weight from the tires if storing for 3 months or more.

How to Prepare Your RV for Storage

Skip the prep steps and the bill arrives later — blown water lines in January, seized slide mechanisms that need a shop visit, or a rodent nest discovered in the engine bay after six months in a lot. The work you do before parking saves far more time and money than it costs.

Clean the exterior thoroughly: Wash the entire exterior including the roof. Remove all bugs, road grime, and mildew before storage. Apply a UV protectant or wax to fiberglass and painted surfaces. Inspect and treat any rubber roof seams or seals with appropriate conditioner to prevent cracking.

Clean and empty the interior: Remove all food, beverages, and organic materials. Wipe down all surfaces, vacuum carpets, and clean upholstery. Leave cabinet doors and compartments slightly open to allow air circulation and prevent mildew.

Drain and flush all water systems: Drain the fresh water tank, water heater, and all water lines. If storing over winter in a cold climate, use RV antifreeze (propylene glycol, not automotive antifreeze) to protect all lines and the water pump from freezing. Drain and flush the black and grey tanks as well.

Stabilize the fuel: Fill the fuel tank to at least 95% to minimize condensation buildup. Add a fuel stabilizer and run the engine for 15 minutes to circulate it through the fuel system. This prevents varnish and deposits from forming in carburetors and injectors.

Change the engine oil and check fluid levels: Old oil is acidic and can cause corrosion during long storage periods. Change the oil and filter before parking. Top off coolant, brake fluid, and power steering fluid.

Disconnect or maintain the battery: Disconnect the house batteries and chassis battery to prevent phantom drain. For long-term storage, connect a battery maintainer (not a basic trickle charger) to keep batteries at full charge without overcharging.

Protect the tires: Inflate tires to the maximum recommended PSI. Park on a hard, level surface rather than grass or dirt to prevent flat spots and reduce moisture wicking into tire sidewalls. Use tire covers to protect against UV degradation. For storage exceeding 3 months, place boards or tire cradles under each tire to reduce flat-spotting.

Cover or seal all openings: Seal the exhaust pipe, air intake, and any exterior vents to prevent rodents and insects from nesting inside. Use foam plugs, mesh covers, or purpose-made vent covers. Place rodent deterrents (steel wool, peppermint sachets, or commercial repellents) inside compartments.

Retract slide-outs and awnings: Store the RV with all slide-outs fully retracted to reduce stress on the seals. Retract awnings fully and secure them. Extended awnings are vulnerable to wind damage and can collect debris that leads to mold.

Check and address the propane: Close the main valve at the tank before moving the RV into any enclosed or covered space. Propane is heavier than air, so even a small leak in an enclosed structure creates an accumulation risk that can ignite from a single spark. Note the fuel level before storage so you are not caught short on the next trip. For storage lasting three months or more, consider removing the tanks entirely and storing them outdoors or in a well-ventilated area rather than leaving them attached. Never store an RV with propane appliances left in pilot or standing flame mode — this applies to furnaces, water heaters, and refrigerators.

Wine Storage Units: Find Climate Controlled Wine Storage Near You

Wine is one of the few things you store that actively changes based on its environment. A bottle stored at the wrong temperature, exposed to too much light, or left standing upright too long can go from excellent to undrinkable before you ever open it.

Whether you are a collector growing out of home storage space or a business managing inventory, a specialist wine storage facility provides the controlled conditions your collection needs. Search storage facilities near you to find climate controlled units that can accommodate wine storage.

Who Needs Wine Storage?

Wine storage is a practical solution for several types of collectors and businesses:

  • Home collectors: Bottles outgrowing the refrigerator, kitchen rack, or home wine fridge
  • Serious collectors: Growing collections requiring consistent long-term cellaring conditions
  • Buyers of futures or cases: Wine purchased to age for years before opening
  • Restaurant and hospitality businesses: Managing wine inventory outside of the establishment
  • Estate and inheritance collections: Properly storing a wine collection during a property transition
  • Apartment and condo dwellers: No basement or cellar space at home for long-term storage

What Types of Wine Need Proper Storage?

Not all wine benefits equally from dedicated storage. These categories have the most to gain:

  • Investment and futures wines: Bottles purchased specifically to appreciate in value require precise long-term conditions to protect that investment
  • Age-worthy reds: Cabernet Sauvignon, Barolo, Bordeaux, and similar structured reds develop complexity over years and decades — proper storage is what makes that aging possible
  • Champagne and sparkling wines: Sensitive to temperature swings and light; premium bottles meant for cellaring should be stored at stable temperatures, not in a standard refrigerator
  • Large collections (50+ bottles): Once a collection grows beyond home storage capacity, a dedicated facility prevents bottles from being stored in unsuitable conditions
  • Everyday drinking wine in quantity: If you buy wine by the case to rotate through over months, climate controlled storage keeps the remaining bottles in good condition between purchases

Wines intended for drinking within the next few weeks do not require dedicated storage. The investment in a storage unit is most worthwhile for bottles you plan to hold for months or years.

What Wine Needs to Stay in Good Condition

Wine is sensitive to four environmental factors. Any facility you use should control all of them.

Temperature

Temperature is the most critical factor. Wine stored too warm oxidizes and turns sour faster. Wine stored too cold can cause the cork to contract and allow air into the bottle. The ideal storage temperature is between 55 and 65 degrees Fahrenheit for most wines. Consistency matters as much as the target number — frequent temperature swings are more damaging than a slightly elevated but stable temperature.

Humidity

The ideal relative humidity for wine storage is between 60 and 70 percent. Too dry and corks dry out and shrink, allowing oxidation. Too humid and mold can grow on labels and corks. Climate controlled storage units that manage both temperature and humidity offer the best protection.

Light

Ultraviolet light degrades wine faster than almost any other external factor. This is why most wine bottles are made from dark-tinted glass. A storage environment with no direct sunlight and minimal artificial UV exposure is essential. Storage units with no exterior windows are ideal. If a unit has light exposure, storing bottles in their original wooden cases or cardboard cartons provides additional protection.

Vibration

Prolonged vibration can disturb sediment in wine, disrupt the slow chemical processes involved in aging, and contribute to a grainier texture and less refined flavor over time. Choose a facility away from heavy traffic, industrial equipment, or frequent foot traffic. While no facility is perfectly vibration-free, some are significantly better than others.

Types of Wine Storage Options

Climate Controlled Storage Units

A standard climate controlled self storage unit keeps temperature and humidity at stable levels year-round. While not purpose-built for wine, these units can be highly effective for collections that will be accessed periodically. Look for facilities that actively monitor and log temperature and humidity and not those that set it and walk away.

Best for: Collectors looking for affordable, accessible storage for medium to large collections. Practical for wines intended for drinking within 5 to 10 years.

Wine Locker or Bin Storage

Some specialty facilities offer individual wine lockers or shared bin storage — smaller dedicated spaces within a larger climate controlled wine vault. These are priced per bin or per case rather than per square foot, making them cost-effective for smaller collections.

Best for: Collectors with fewer than 50 to 100 bottles who do not need a full storage unit. Also useful for those who want to separate part of a collection from home storage.

Specialty Wine Vaults

Dedicated wine storage facilities go beyond temperature and humidity control to include vibration dampening, individual locked cages or rooms, and inventory management services. These exist primarily in larger urban markets. Availability varies by market and is less common than general climate controlled storage. When searching, filter for climate controlled facilities and contact them directly to ask about wine-specific storage capabilities.

Best for: High-value collections, wine invested for long-term aging, or collectors who want full inventory tracking and professional management.

How to Store Wine Correctly

Even with the right facility, how the wine is arranged matters. Store bottles on their side. Horizontal storage keeps the cork in contact with the wine, preventing it from drying out and shrinking. Upright storage for extended periods risks oxidation as the cork dries. Most purpose-built wine racks and facility shelving automatically orient bottles correctly.

Keep wines isolated from strong odors. Wine breathes slowly through the cork. Chemical smells, including cleaning products, paint, solvents, or strong food odors, can be absorbed and affect the taste over time. Store only wine in a wine storage unit and avoid facilities where adjacent units store chemicals.

Do not store wine near old or rotting wood. Degraded wood produces compounds that can lead to cork taint, giving wine a musty, cardboard-like off-flavor.

Keep the collection organized. Label or track bottles by variety, producer, vintage, and intended drinking window. Regularly retrieving bottles and disturbing the rest of the collection increases vibration and temperature fluctuation.

Wine Storage FAQs

What temperature should wine be stored at?
Between 55 and 65 degrees Fahrenheit is the ideal range for most wines. Consistent temperature is more important than hitting a specific number. Frequent swings between warm and cool are more damaging than a slightly elevated but stable temperature.
Do I need a specialty wine storage facility or will a climate controlled storage unit work?
A well-maintained climate controlled self storage unit can be sufficient for most collectors, particularly for wines intended for drinking within 5 to 10 years. For high-value aged wines or very long-term cellaring, a dedicated wine vault with active monitoring, humidity control, and vibration dampening is the safer choice.
How should wine be oriented in storage?
Bottles should be stored on their side so the cork stays in contact with the wine. This prevents the cork from drying out and contracting, which would allow air into the bottle. Most purpose-built wine racks automatically store bottles horizontally.
Can I store wine in a regular self storage unit?
A standard unclimate-controlled unit is not suitable for wine, as temperature swings will degrade the wine over time. A climate controlled self storage unit can work for short to medium-term storage if the facility actively maintains a stable temperature and humidity within the recommended ranges.

Costs range from $10 to $30 per case per month for wine locker or bin storage, up to $75 to $400 or more per month for a private climate controlled unit depending on size and location. Specialty wine vaults in urban markets can charge $20 to $60 per case per month with full management services.

Types of Motorcycle Storage Units

Enclosed (Indoor) Motorcycle Storage

Fully enclosed storage places your motorcycle inside a lockable drive-up unit with a roll-up door. This is the highest-protection option, keeping the bike safe from weather, dust, pests, and temperature extremes.

Best for: Winter storage in cold or wet climates, classic and vintage bikes, and any motorcycle where paint, chrome, or mechanical condition matters.

Enclosed units are sized in the same range as standard self storage. A 5' x 10' unit comfortably fits most motorcycles with room for a helmet and riding gear. A 10' x 10' gives additional space for tools or maintenance work alongside the bike.

Covered Motorcycle Storage

Covered storage parks your motorcycle under a roof structure with open sides. You get protection from rain, hail, and direct sun without the cost of a fully enclosed unit.

Best for: Riders in mild climates who want basic weather protection and flexible, frequent access at a lower price point.

Limitations: Open sides mean limited protection from wind-driven rain, temperature extremes, and pests.

Outdoor Motorcycle Storage

An open, secured lot with perimeter fencing and surveillance cameras. No weather protection, but access is typically easy and pricing is lowest.

Best for: Short-term storage in mild climates, or as a budget option for a bike with a quality weatherproof motorcycle cover.

How to Prepare Your Motorcycle for Storage

Neglected bikes develop problems fast: carburetors seize from varnished fuel, batteries drain completely and won't hold a charge, tires develop flat spots under static weight for months, and chains corrode into stiff, rust-spotted links. The steps below prevent all of that.

Take a final ride to warm up the engine: Before preparing for storage, ride the motorcycle for at least 30 minutes to bring the engine up to full operating temperature. This burns off moisture from the engine internals and helps circulate fresh fluids.

Add fuel stabilizer and fill the tank: Add a fuel stabilizer to the gas tank, then fill the tank completely. Run the engine for 10 minutes to circulate the stabilizer through the fuel system. A full tank minimizes air space and prevents moisture condensation inside the tank. This step is essential for any storage period over 30 days.

Change the oil if it is due: If an oil change is approaching, do it now while the engine is hot. Old oil contains acidic byproducts that cause corrosion when left sitting. Fresh oil during storage is significantly better for engine internals than oil that has been in the engine for thousands of miles.

Wash and wax the motorcycle: Wash the entire motorcycle to remove dirt, bugs, road salt, and grime. Apply a coat of wax to painted surfaces and a metal protectant or light oil to chrome and bare metal parts. Clean and treat leather seats with a conditioner to prevent cracking.

Lubricate the chain: If your motorcycle has a chain drive, clean and lubricate it thoroughly before storage. A properly lubricated chain resists rust and corrosion far better than a dry one sitting for months.

Check and inflate tires: Inflate tires to the manufacturer's maximum recommended PSI. Tires lose pressure slowly during storage and can develop flat spots under prolonged static weight. Parking the bike on a paddock stand keeps weight off the tires entirely and is the best option for long-term storage.

Disconnect the battery or connect a maintainer: Disconnect the negative terminal to prevent phantom drain from electronics. For storage over 30 days, connect a motorcycle battery maintainer (not a basic trickle charger) to keep the battery at full charge without overcharging. Store the battery in a cool, dry place if removing it from the bike.

Cover the exhaust and air intake: Use a rag, foam plug, or purpose-made cover to seal the exhaust pipe and air intake opening. This prevents moisture and pests from entering the engine. Mark these covers visibly so you remember to remove them before starting.

Cover the motorcycle: Use a breathable motorcycle-specific cover. Avoid non-breathable plastic tarps, which trap condensation and promote rust. A good motorcycle cover keeps dust off the bike while allowing moisture to escape.

Getting Your Motorcycle Ready to Ride After Storage

Before your first ride of the season, take these steps:

  • Reconnect the battery and check for corrosion on the terminals
  • Remove exhaust and intake covers
  • Check all fluid levels (coolant, brake fluid, clutch fluid) and top off if needed
  • Check tire pressure and inspect for cracking or flat spots
  • Check the chain lubrication
  • Start the engine and let it idle for several minutes
  • Take a short, slow test ride before going on a long trip
